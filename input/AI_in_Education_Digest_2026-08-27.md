### 1. Department of Education Issues New Guidance on Education Technology and Screen Time

1. **Title:** Department of Education Issues New Guidance on Education Technology and Screen Time
2. **Publication date:** 20 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** THE Journal (1105 Media; K–12 education-technology news outlet), reporting on a U.S. Department of Education "Dear Colleague Letter"
4. **Author(s):** Rhea Kelly
5. **Author note:** Kelly is an editor at THE Journal and its sister publication Campus Technology, covering education technology; the underlying guidance was issued by Kirsten Baesler, assistant secretary of the U.S. Department of Education's Office of Elementary and Secondary Education.
6. **URL:** https://thejournal.com/articles/2026/08/20/department-of-education-issues-new-guidance-on-education-technology-and-screen-time.aspx (primary guidance: https://www.ed.gov/media/document/guidance-education-technology-and-screentime-schools-114386.pdf)
7. **Summary:**
The article reports that on 20 August 2026 the U.S. Department of Education issued a Dear Colleague Letter outlining new guidance on the educational value of technology in schools. According to the article, the letter argues that "screen time" should not be the primary measure of whether education technology is beneficial; the central question, it states, should be whether technology measurably improves teaching, learning, and student outcomes. The article reports that the guidance distinguishes excessive recreational screen use from education technology aligned with clear instructional goals, and quotes Baesler stating that education technology "can strengthen teaching, deepen learning, and expand education opportunity when aligned with clear instructional goals and implemented effectively."

The article summarizes the letter's recommendation that investments in education technology be guided by evidence of effectiveness alongside cost, usability, accessibility, interoperability, implementation support, and educator experience. It lists the guidance's expectations that providers publish rigorous independent evaluations of product impact, share implementation guidance, provide transparent information about product capabilities and limitations, and continuously improve products using outcome evidence. It also enumerates considerations for states and districts, including distinguishing recreational from instructional technology policies, evaluating tools on demonstrated learning outcomes rather than screen time alone, incorporating evidence of effectiveness (including RCTs) into procurement and renewal decisions, establishing review processes, and supporting professional learning for educators. The article states that decisions on how to best use education technology should be made by states and local communities with input from parents and teachers.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, the guidance's central claim is that education technology should be judged by its demonstrated educational value and effect on student outcomes rather than by screen time alone, and that recreational and instructional technology should be treated as distinct. It states that procurement and renewal decisions should be evidence-based, that providers should supply independent evaluations and transparent reporting, and that authority over education-technology use should rest with states and local communities with parent and teacher input.

### 2. The learning curve for AI in schools

1. **Title:** The learning curve for AI in schools
2. **Publication date:** 20 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The Christian Science Monitor ("The Monitor's View" editorial)
4. **Author(s):** The Monitor's Editorial Board
5. **Author note:** The Monitor's Editorial Board is the unsigned institutional editorial voice of The Christian Science Monitor, a national news outlet with an education desk.
6. **URL:** https://www.csmonitor.com/Editorials/the-monitors-view/2026/0820/The-learning-curve-for-AI-in-schools
7. **Summary:**
The editorial observes that as students return to classrooms, schools are being offered AI-powered products intended to improve learning, noting that over the summer Google and Anthropic both launched technologies designed for teachers, joining offerings from OpenAI and Khan Academy, and that more than 30 states have adopted guidelines on AI use in education. It reports that a large majority of high schoolers already use AI for schoolwork and that the percentage of teachers using AI tools rose from 32% to 61% over the prior year. It states that adopting the technology will likely help teachers with basic administrative tasks, while much of the research indicates students benefit when they bring their own judgment about the limits and dangers of AI, such as biased answers or privacy concerns.

The editorial cites a survey by the nonprofit Common Sense Media reporting that about one-quarter of teens have asked a teacher how to judge the accuracy of an AI summary, that most teens view AI as both an answer machine and a support for their thinking, and that two-thirds say it helps them understand lessons rather than only complete them faster. It highlights that at least half of teens acknowledge skills AI cannot or should not replace, such as "working well with others" and "fact-checking and critical thinking." The piece then draws on Howard Gardner of Harvard University, quoting his prediction that many cognitive aspects of mind may be performed by large language systems while aspects of respect and ethics should remain anchored in human teaching, and his comment to Education Week that AI might push humans toward higher expressions of intelligence only if individuals are intrinsically motivated to stretch their abilities.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The editorial's central claim is that the rapid rush to adopt AI in schools is prompting new reflection on what kinds of intelligence students and teachers must bring to the AI era, and that the forms of intelligence most valuable and least replaceable — collaboration, ethical reasoning, fact-checking, and critical thinking — are those best cultivated through human teaching. It frames the key question for schools as whether AI can foster shared understanding and the capacity of young people to work together on ethical and moral issues.

### 4. How Should AI Shape Teacher Education?

1. **Title:** How Should AI Shape Teacher Education?
2. **Publication date:** 26 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Week (Teaching & Learning / Teaching Profession), reporting on a new framework from the American Association of Colleges for Teacher Education (AACTE)
4. **Author(s):** Sarah D. Sparks
5. **Author note:** Sparks is an assistant editor at Education Week who covers the teaching profession and pedagogy for the outlet.
6. **URL:** https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/how-should-ai-shape-teacher-education/2026/08 (AACTE framework document: https://aacte.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/AACTE-AI-Framework-082026-1.pdf)
7. **Summary:**
The article reports that the American Association of Colleges for Teacher Education (AACTE), which represents more than 500 public and private college and university programs, released a new framework calling on university-based teacher-education programs to build a comprehensive approach to preparing teachers to understand how AI can affect K–12 education. It states that the framework, developed by a task force of members and outside experts, sets out technical skills, pedagogical knowledge, and ethical judgment that teachers need in order to use and teach AI, but does not include specific curriculum examples or standards and is intended as a starting point for further discussion. According to the article, the framework calls for explicit preservice training in four areas: ethics and policy (including AI literacy and equitable access); practice and implementation (including evaluating student work produced with AI tools); cognitive issues (including biases and hallucinations in the technology and effects on students' critical thinking); and professional expertise (including teacher judgment and teacher-student relationships).

The article quotes contributors to the framework, including Anne Tapp Jaksa of the University of Michigan, Carole Basile of Arizona State University, and Peña Bedesem of Kent State University, on how AI intersects with planning, assessment, differentiation, and professional ethics, and on the view that AI training should not become "just a computer science course." It reports that AACTE also proposes that aspiring teachers who object to the technology on privacy, accessibility, cultural, pedagogical, or ethical grounds be allowed to refuse to use it and still earn a teaching credential — a proposal the article notes could be difficult for states to implement. It situates the framework alongside federal policy, noting that university programs prepare more than 90% of new educators and that much of the more than $1.7 billion in Title II teacher-training grants now prioritizes preparing educators to use AI, citing an April 2025 executive order on AI-based teacher training.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports AACTE's central claim that teacher-preparation programs should adopt a comprehensive, structured approach to AI — spanning technical skills, pedagogy, cognitive considerations, and ethics — rather than treating it as an isolated technical topic or leaving training to individual districts and vendors. It also presents AACTE's position that teacher candidates should retain the right to decline to use AI on legitimate grounds without being barred from licensure.
