### 1. Generative AI Literacies and Discipline-Specific Knowledge: Preservice Teachers Planning Task-Based L2 Lessons with GenAI

1. **Title:** Generative AI Literacies and Discipline-Specific Knowledge: Preservice Teachers Planning Task-Based L2 Lessons with GenAI
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-12
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Digital Studies in Language and Literature (De Gruyter Brill)
4. **Author(s):** Nicola Brocca; Anna Romano
5. **Author note:** The authors are language-teacher-education researchers, with Brocca listed at the University of Innsbruck, Austria.
6. **URL:** https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/dsll-2026-0045/html
7. **Summary:**
This exploratory case study followed ten preservice teachers of Italian, working in five pairs within a compulsory BA seminar, as they used a university platform built on GPT-4o to plan task-based lessons for beginner (A1/A2) learners. The data were lesson plans written before the module, chat transcripts, the AI-assisted plans and the students' written reflections, analysed with qualitative content analysis by two raters.

Most AI-assisted plans met the task-based criteria of a clear outcome and an information gap. The criterion missed most often was letting learners draw on their own language resources, because target forms were fixed in advance. All pairs wrote subject-specific prompts, and four of the five pairs refined their prompts over several rounds.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors report that a stronger grasp of task-based teaching went together with more purposeful use of the AI tool, and they conclude that the tool works best when teachers can critically evaluate and adapt its output rather than treating it as a substitute for pedagogical expertise.
9. **Validity:** Peer-reviewed journal article; qualitative case study with a very small sample (n=10); the course instructor was also a rater. Setting is initial teacher education, not school pupils.

### 2. So much chat about GenAI – but we must know more about what's happening in our classrooms now

1. **Title:** So much chat about GenAI – but we must know more about what's happening in our classrooms now
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-11
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** EduResearch Matters (Australian Association for Research in Education blog)
4. **Author(s):** Lina Markauskaite; Natasha Arthars; Hongzhi (Veronica) Yang; Danny Liu; Matthew Hill
5. **Author note:** Markauskaite, Arthars, Yang and Liu are learning-sciences and educational-technology researchers at the University of Sydney, and Hill is Director of The Barker Institute, working in a school-university research partnership.
6. **URL:** https://blog.aare.edu.au/2026/11/11/so-much-chat-about-genai-but-we-must-know-more-about-whats-happening-in-our-classrooms-now/
7. **Summary:**
The post reports on the authors' rapid literature review of empirical research on generative AI and young people's learning, covering 271 peer-reviewed papers from more than 45 countries in PreK-12 settings. Most studies were quantitative or quasi-experimental, set in high schools, and focused on language, mathematics, science and IT. Only four were conducted in Australian schools.

The reviewed studies often report higher engagement and motivation and lower anxiety, together with new self-regulation demands such as resisting ready-made answers. Outcomes were better for students with more prior knowledge, when custom tools were combined with scaffolding, and with teacher support. Ethics, collaboration and the mechanisms behind effects were rarely studied.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors describe the evidence as fragmented, with few clear answers. They recommend distinguishing learning from, with, about and through configuring AI, aligning assessment with the tools students used, broadening theory and methods, and building sustained school-based research partnerships with participatory research agendas.
9. **Validity:** Blog post by academics summarising their own literature review (institutional research summary); the full review was not opened and individual study sample sizes are not given.

### 3. School leaders and EdTech: a leaders' roundtable on challenges, possibilities and capacity

1. **Title:** School leaders and EdTech: a leaders' roundtable on challenges, possibilities and capacity
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-11
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Chartered College of Teaching (news and blogs)
4. **Author(s):** Helen Barker
5. **Author note:** Barker is Teacher Assessment Manager at the Chartered College of Teaching, England's professional body for teachers; the roundtable was chaired by Dame Alison Peacock.
6. **URL:** https://chartered.college/news-blogs/school-leaders-and-edtech-a-leaders-roundtable-on-challenges-possibilities-and-capacity/
7. **Summary:**
The post reports a roundtable of school leaders from early years, primary and secondary settings in England. Leaders described a system under strain from the upcoming Curriculum and Assessment Review and the 2026 Schools White Paper, falling rolls and budgets, rising demand for special educational needs support, and administrative work that crowds out school development.

Leaders saw generative AI as a possible way to reduce administrative load, for example by summarising documents. Many said they lacked the capacity to judge how AI would help in their context, and they stressed that use should be purposeful and keep the human side of teaching central.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The roundtable concluded that limited capacity, not unwillingness, constrains technology adoption. Leaders asked for clearer information on what technology can realistically do, a coordinating role for the Department for Education through shared practice and communities of practice, and technology decisions taken together with curriculum and SEND reforms.
9. **Validity:** Write-up of a practitioner roundtable by a professional body; no original data. AI is one strand of a broader ed-tech discussion.

### 6. Different Schools, Different Futures: Education in the Singularity

1. **Title:** Different Schools, Different Futures: Education in the Singularity
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-10
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Disrupted: Teaching and Learning in An AI World (Substack)
4. **Author(s):** Stefan Bauschard
5. **Author note:** Bauschard is an educator who writes a newsletter and co-hosts a podcast on AI and schooling, and works in competitive debate education.
6. **URL:** https://stefanbauschard.substack.com/p/different-schools-different-futures
7. **Summary:**
Bauschard argues that schools treat AI as a one-time implementation problem while waiting for a stability that he expects will not arrive. He cites RAND survey findings that fewer than half of principals reported a school or district AI policy, a 2023 survey of AI researchers on timelines for machine capability, and a University of Reading study in which most AI-written exam answers went undetected, and he questions whether current assignments still serve their purpose.

He expects schooling to diversify into homeschooling, microschools, specialised schools and public schools that coordinate courses and mentors. He warns that this could reproduce the inequities of tracking, citing RAND figures showing that low-poverty districts were more likely than high-poverty districts to have trained teachers on AI.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The author claims that schools should prepare for several institutional models rather than one settled model, explain the purpose behind AI policies and assignments, protect foundational knowledge, and monitor equity of access to new pathways.
9. **Validity:** Opinion essay drawing on secondary sources; no original data. Directly about K-12 policy.

### 7. AI in Education #15: Five Things I Learnt from Bibi Groot about the good, the bad and the ugly of testing an AI tutor

1. **Title:** AI in Education #15: Five Things I Learnt from Bibi Groot about the good, the bad and the ugly of testing an AI tutor
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-10
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Eedi Newsletter (Substack)
4. **Author(s):** Craig Barton
5. **Author note:** Barton is a UK maths teacher, author and podcast host who works with Eedi, a maths diagnostic platform.
6. **URL:** https://eedi.substack.com/p/ai-in-education-15-five-things-i-096
7. **Summary:**
Barton summarises Eedi's second randomised trial of an AI maths tutor, run with Google DeepMind with 1,179 students aged 12 to 15 in ten UK state schools over about ten weeks. Students were assigned to standard remediation, an expert human tutor, an AI tutor, or an AI tutor given extra student context, and expert tutors reviewed every AI message before students saw it.

Within lessons, tutored students corrected a similar question about 57% of the time against 43% with standard remediation, and the AI matched the human tutors. There was no difference between groups on the Star Maths standardised test, which the post links to tutored students completing fewer study units. The AI checked understanding in 91% of conversations against 60% for human tutors, and tutors approved 94.7% of AI drafts unchanged but found supervising the AI less enjoyable. A larger trial with about 15,000 students in 26 schools is announced.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post claims that supervised AI tutoring can match expert human tutoring on immediate correction with few safety problems, that broad attainment gains did not appear within ten weeks, and that short trials judged on broad end-of-year tests may give a misleading picture.
9. **Validity:** Practitioner newsletter summarising a vendor-partner technical report that is not peer-reviewed; the author has a commercial connection to Eedi. K-12 secondary mathematics. The underlying report is dated 2026-10-29, before the window.

### 8. The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A November 2026 Review

1. **Title:** The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A November 2026 Review
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-10
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** SCALE Initiative, Stanford Graduate School of Education
4. **Author(s):** Seth Walker; Lily Fesler; JP Martinez Claeys; Chris Agnew; Susanna Loeb
5. **Author note:** The authors are researchers at Stanford's SCALE Initiative; Loeb is an education economist who leads its research on AI in K-12 schools.
6. **URL:** https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/evidence-base-review/evidence-base-ai-k12-november-review
7. **Summary:**
This update to a March 2026 review covers randomised and quasi-experimental studies of generative AI published between October 2025 and June 2026. An LLM performed a first screen of new papers, and at least two human reviewers coded candidates against What Works Clearinghouse standards. Thirty-two papers met the bar, bringing the causal evidence base to 52 papers, most on student-facing AI. Five new student studies were set in U.S. K-12 schools, while most new studies were postsecondary.

For students, the review finds that later unaided performance can fall when AI produces the work, that AI used as learning support gives mixed results, that AI often improves how learning feels without improving outcomes, and that purpose-built tools are not reliably better than general chatbots. For educators, AI reduced lesson-preparation time without detected loss of quality and increased the amount of feedback, while one teaching-support tool reduced student motivation and lowered scores for students of lower-performing teachers.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors conclude that effects depend on design and use, with more promising results when AI supports learning or specific educator tasks than when it replaces student thinking or teacher judgement. They call for K-12 studies of learning after AI is removed, transfer, equity and educator-facing tools.
9. **Validity:** University research-institute evidence review, not peer-reviewed; much of the underlying evidence consists of preprints, and the LLM screen may have missed studies.

### 9. Scoring consistency of teachers and artificial intelligence algorithms in written expression assessment: a comparative quantitative study

1. **Title:** Scoring consistency of teachers and artificial intelligence algorithms in written expression assessment: a comparative quantitative study
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-10
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Scientific Reports (Nature Portfolio)
4. **Author(s):** Ahmet Akçay; Kılıç Köçeri; Tuğba Tümen; Serdar Safalı
5. **Author note:** Akçay is in the Department of Turkish Language Education, Faculty of Education, Ağrı İbrahim Çeçen University, Turkey.
6. **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-74899-1
7. **Summary:**
In this study, 122 students in grades 6 to 8 at a Turkish public school each wrote one text in a randomly assigned genre (folktale, poetry or biography). Teachers and four LLM tools (ChatGPT, Grok, Minimax and Gemini) scored all texts with the same five-dimension rubric, and agreement was measured with correlation and intraclass correlation statistics.

ChatGPT agreed most closely with the teachers and Minimax least. Genre had no overall effect, but teachers scored poetry lower while the AI tools did not. A gender difference in scores appeared only in the teachers' scoring.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors conclude that LLM tools, ChatGPT in particular, may be suitable for low-stakes formative feedback on student writing but cannot replace human judgement in high-stakes writing assessment.
9. **Validity:** Peer-reviewed journal article (accepted version); comparative correlational design in one school and one language; the number of teacher raters and model versions are not reported.

### 13. Middle School Students and Generative AI Solving Exponent Problems: A Comparative Analysis of Written Solutions and Solution Reliability

1. **Title:** Middle School Students and Generative AI Solving Exponent Problems: A Comparative Analysis of Written Solutions and Solution Reliability
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-06
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Research Square (preprint; under review at Digital Experiences in Mathematics Education)
4. **Author(s):** Sena Gencel; İclal Sude Çetinkaya; Fatmagül Ergün
5. **Author note:** The authors work in mathematics education; affiliations are not shown on the preprint page.
6. **URL:** https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11239535/v1
7. **Summary:**
The study compares handwritten solutions from 22 students aged 12 to 13 with the first responses of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok to the same ten exponent problems, using a comparative case-study design and thematic analysis.

Student solutions were brief and symbolic, left reasoning implicit, and contained errors clustered around exponent rules, decimals and negative signs. AI outputs were more explicit, and AI errors appeared in visual and contextual tasks; no errors were coded for ChatGPT, while the other three tools made some. Some student and AI solutions reached correct answers through flawed processes.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors conclude that AI-generated solutions should be checked for representation, strategy, execution and justification rather than accepted as finished answers.
9. **Validity:** Preprint, not peer-reviewed; small qualitative comparison using a first-response protocol with specific model versions. Date taken from the posted date in the preprint page metadata.

### 16. Thinking Is Not Writing: Designing AI Chatbots to Connect Reasoning and Revision

1. **Title:** Thinking Is Not Writing: Designing AI Chatbots to Connect Reasoning and Revision
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-05
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** arXiv (cs.HC), arXiv:2611.07792
4. **Author(s):** Lucile Favero; Tanja Käser; Juan Antonio Pérez-Ortiz; Nuria Oliver
5. **Author note:** The authors work on human-centred AI and educational technology; affiliations are not shown on the abstract page.
6. **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07792
7. **Summary:**
The paper presents Maike, a Socratic chatbot for argumentative writing that diagnoses students' arguments, asks targeted questions, keeps a model of the student across "Think" and "Write" modes, and does not generate essay text.

In a classroom study, 65 secondary-school students used either Maike or a control chatbot with the same interface, language model and modes. About 40% of students improved their essay scores. 8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors conclude that AI writing support should connect reflection with revision, and they contribute a system architecture and a method for linking interaction patterns to writing outcomes.
9. **Validity:** Preprint, not peer-reviewed; small classroom comparison (n=65). Student age and country are not stated in the abstract, and the improvement figure is not broken down by condition there.

### 17. Personal, Academic and Professional Characteristics Associated with Perceptions of Artificial Intelligence Among Practising and Prospective Teachers

2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-05
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Research Square (preprint)

6. **URL:** https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11610737/v1

9. **Validity:** Preprint, not peer-reviewed; cross-sectional self-report survey. Date taken from the page's citation metadata rather than visible text.

### 19. The Latest Findings in AI and Learning - November 2026

1. **Title:** The Latest Findings in AI and Learning - November 2026
2. **Publication date:** 2026-11-04
