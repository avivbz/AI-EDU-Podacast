### 1. Parenting in an AI revolution

1. **Title:** Parenting in an AI revolution (No More Sidewalks: Parenting Today, episode 5)
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-29
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Brookings Institution (podcast, with transcript)
4. **Author(s):** Abby McCloskey (host); Jonathan Haidt (guest)
5. **Author note:** McCloskey is an economist and policy writer; Haidt is a social psychologist at NYU Stern who writes on children, technology and mental health.
6. **URL:** https://www.brookings.edu/articles/parenting-in-an-ai-revolution/
7. **Summary:**
Haidt traces a sequence from smartphones and social media, through classroom devices and ed-tech during COVID-era remote learning, to AI, which he describes as the largest disruption to childhood so far. He names cognitive offloading and emotional reliance on chatbots as the new risks, and attributes declining NAEP and PISA scores since about 2012 to screen-based schooling.

The recommendations cover schools and homes: full-day phone-free schools, removing one-to-one devices from elementary classrooms, ending computer-based state testing, age limits of 16 for social media and 18 for companion-style AI, and more unsupervised play. The host cites survey figures on teen chatbot use; Haidt refers to studies in which students did better with AI but worse once it was removed, without naming them.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Haidt states that the harms of screens and relational AI to children are real rather than a moral panic, and that parents, schools and policymakers should restrict phones, classroom devices and companion-style AI now.
9. **Validity:** Opinion/advocacy podcast with transcript. Statistics are the speakers' own and are not sourced on the page; K-12 and AI relevance is partial because much of the discussion concerns phones and social media.

### 2. Free readers, free people: What schools should protect

1. **Title:** Free readers, free people: What schools should protect
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-28
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Thomas B. Fordham Institute (2026 Wonkathon commentary)
4. **Author(s):** Ila Deshmukh Towery
5. **Author note:** Towery is a Partner at Education First, an education consulting organisation.
6. **URL:** https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/free-readers-free-people-what-schools-should-protect
7. **Summary:**
Towery argues that literacy is a form of freedom and that technology rules in schools should be built around what schools want to protect rather than only what they want to prevent. She notes declining U.S. reading scores and says AI-generated text raises the demand for critical judgment, because output can look credible without being accurate.

She names three things to protect. For attention, she recommends print-first reading in early grades and for long texts, limited screens in elementary school and gradual loosening in high school with teacher training in evaluating AI output. For relationships, she favours discussion-based learning, tools that prompt students to explain and revise, and resistance to AI companion features. For trust, she calls for informing families about tools and data collection, honesty over punitive surveillance because AI detectors are unreliable, and vendor accountability through evidence requirements and outcomes-based contracts.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** School technology rules, including those on AI, should protect attention, relationships and trust, and should be decided through deliberation among educators, families and communities.
9. **Validity:** Opinion/commentary by an education professional; draws on secondary sources and does not present original data.

### 3. Every school already has a technology policy. Student learning should guide it.

1. **Title:** Every school already has a technology policy. Student learning should guide it.
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-27
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Thomas B. Fordham Institute (2026 Wonkathon commentary)
4. **Author(s):** Bailey Cato Czupryk
5. **Author note:** Czupryk is Chief Research & Design Officer at Leading Educators, a U.S. teacher-development nonprofit.
6. **URL:** https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/every-school-already-has-technology-policy-student-learning-should-guide-it
7. **Summary:**
The essay argues that every school already makes technology decisions, written down or not, and proposes one test: whether a tool increases the time students spend on rigorous content with strong teachers. Tools that pass should be piloted first and used only if they do.

It sets out four rules. States should define technology categories by instructional purpose and require evidence of whether a tool works, for whom and in what context. Districts should audit existing tools, cut redundancy and keep short approved lists, and should pair any purchase with teacher training and coaching, since teacher knowledge predicted changed practice in the author's pilots. The author also proposes that tool contracts lapse unless districts can show learning gains.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** School technology governance, including for AI tools, should rest on evidence of instructional benefit and on teacher capacity rather than on vendor claims.
9. **Validity:** Opinion/commentary by an education professional; uses secondary data and informal observations from the author's own pilots.

### 4. Subsidiarity as a guiding ed-tech principle

1. **Title:** Subsidiarity as a guiding ed-tech principle
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-27
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Thomas B. Fordham Institute (2026 Wonkathon commentary)
4. **Author(s):** Josiah Padley
5. **Author note:** Padley is an Education Policy Fellow at American Experiment and a former high school teacher.
6. **URL:** https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/subsidiarity-guiding-ed-tech-principle
7. **Summary:**
Padley argues that school-issued devices and AI have pulled students off task and that schools spend much of the day on low-value screen time. He proposes subsidiarity, meaning the most local competent authority decides, as the organising principle for ed-tech governance.

Under this approach, states give research-based guidance rather than mandates and push for tools with administrative controls, such as limiting AI by grade, class period or task. Districts write grade-by-grade technology plans with principals and teachers, consider shared laptop carts over one-to-one devices, use more pen-and-paper assessment, and publish usage data. He calls blanket product bans blunt.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Guidance rather than mandates, with local decisions and transparent data, is the better way to govern devices and AI in schools.
9. **Validity:** Opinion/commentary by a former teacher and policy fellow; claims about student behaviour rest on linked sources and are not original data.

### 5. Evaluate the product team, not just the product

1. **Title:** Evaluate the product team, not just the product
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-27
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Thomas B. Fordham Institute (2026 Wonkathon commentary)
4. **Author(s):** Emily Freitag
5. **Author note:** Freitag is cofounder and CEO of Instruction Partners, a U.S. school-improvement nonprofit that has studied classroom use of student-facing AI tools.
6. **URL:** https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/evaluate-product-team-not-just-product
7. **Summary:**
Freitag argues that districts buying AI-powered instructional tools are choosing a long-term partner as well as software, because the technology changes quickly and the product team's behaviour determines whether it improves for students over a contract's life. She draws on Instruction Partners' AI in Action Learning Tour.

She offers five questions for developers: how often senior product leaders visit classrooms and what changes follow, which popular feature the team removed because it did not support learning, how it has improved feedback to students, what high-use teachers with strong outcomes do differently, and what district customisations it has made or will refuse.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Strong product partners have a grounded point of view, evidence of impact, awareness that implementation matters as much as design, and respect for educators; teachers remain central to instruction.
9. **Validity:** Opinion/commentary based on the author's practitioner experience; no systematic evidence presented.

### 6. Peers4U: Designing Learner-Centered Virtual Peers for Children's Multimodal Mathematics Learning

1. **Title:** Peers4U: Designing Learner-Centered Virtual Peers for Children's Multimodal Mathematics Learning
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-27
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** arXiv (cs.HC), arXiv:2610.37238
4. **Author(s):** Zhi Zheng, Binglin Liu, Weihao Chen, Lan Luo, Yu Mei, Jie Cai, Chun Yu, Yuanchun Shi
5. **Author note:** Not stated on the abstract page; the authors work on human-computer interaction systems for learning.
6. **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37238
7. **Summary:**
The paper presents Peers4U, a system of LLM-driven virtual peers meant to help young children understand early mathematics. The peers adapt to each child's cognitive level, present solutions in several modes with visible reasoning steps, and hold mixed-initiative conversations that follow a teaching progression.

In a study with 22 children and two experts, topic orchestration centred on the learner outperformed a persona-centred baseline. Children spontaneously created new artefacts to share strategies and produced more solutions when grouped with more "Leading Peers." The children's ages and school setting are not stated in the abstract.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors offer design insights for learner-centred, multimodal virtual-peer systems; they do not claim learning gains.
9. **Validity:** Preprint, not peer reviewed; small design-oriented user study (22 children, 2 experts). K-12 setting is not confirmed.

### 7. When AI Helps Students Learn Less: What the Research Reveals

1. **Title:** When AI Helps Students Learn Less: What the Research Reveals
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-25
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** AI in Education Podcast (Libsyn)
4. **Author(s):** Dan Bowen and Ray Fleming
5. **Author note:** Bowen and Fleming host a podcast tracking AI policy and deployments in education; the page states the views are their own.
6. **URL:** https://aiineducationpodcast.libsyn.com/when-ai-helps-students-learn-less-what-the-research-reveals
7. **Summary:**
The episode asks whether AI improves learning or only makes tasks easier. It reviews five research studies on AI-assisted grading, assessment timing, student motivation, reliance on AI and independent performance, and Socratic-style AI tutors. The show notes say that more questioning by an AI tutor does not necessarily improve outcomes and that some students became more frustrated and performed worse.

The notes also cover news items: a university's Gemini rollout, Common Sense Media's finding that ChatGPT's teen protections are lacking, a newspaper report on Australian universities using AI to mark student work, and Microsoft support for vocational educators. They add that AI grading should be benchmarked against human-to-human marking consistency.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The notes present the view that better task output does not equal better learning, that reliance on AI may reduce persistence, and that AI-grading claims need a human-reliability baseline.
9. **Validity:** Podcast commentary on research, summarised from show notes only (audio not heard). Most studies discussed concern higher education or programming rather than K-12, per the show notes' links.

### 8. K-12 AI literacy frameworks after generative AI: A scoping review

1. **Title:** K-12 AI literacy frameworks after generative AI: A scoping review
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-25
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** International Journal of Professional Development, Learners and Learning, 8(2), e2618
4. **Author(s):** Med Kharbach, Johanathan Woodworth, Sandra Sawchuk
5. **Author note:** The authors are at Mount Saint Vincent University, Halifax, Canada, and research AI literacy and teacher education.
6. **URL:** https://www.ijpdll.com/article/k-12-ai-literacy-frameworks-after-generative-ai-a-scoping-review-19665
7. **Summary:**
The authors map framework-level work on K-12 AI literacy published from January 2024 to December 2025, following JBI scoping-review guidance and PRISMA-ScR reporting. After screening 204 unique records, 50 studies from 15 countries were included; the United States, Hong Kong and mainland China account for 70%.

Frameworks describe AI literacy in cognitive, affective, behavioural, ethical and social terms, but labels are inconsistent and rarely mapped to one another. Generative-AI skills such as checking outputs, spotting hallucinations and prompting receive little attention, as do socioeconomic status, disability and linguistic diversity. Assessment research and curriculum research have developed largely separately.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The field is fragmented; the authors call for a shared vocabulary, curriculum-aligned assessment, longitudinal studies and validation across more diverse contexts.
9. **Validity:** Peer-reviewed scoping review (50 included studies); a mapping of frameworks, not an effect-size synthesis. Page lists a submission date of 2 June 2026 and no accepted date.

### 9. Preparing Our Children For A Crazy AI World

1. **Title:** Preparing Our Children For A Crazy AI World
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-24
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Forbes (contributor column)
4. **Author(s):** Dan Fitzpatrick
5. **Author note:** Fitzpatrick is a speaker and writer on AI in education who also sells AI-for-educators guides and courses.
6. **URL:** https://www.forbes.com/sites/danfitzpatrick/2026/10/24/preparing-our-children-for-a-crazy-ai-world/
7. **Summary:**
Fitzpatrick argues that steering children toward narrow, "sensible" career paths may limit them as AI reshapes work. He contends that broad interests and deep expertise can coexist, and that as AI takes over more tasks, young people will need judgment about what is worth doing, which draws on wide knowledge.

He says school subjects suit timetabling but real projects, such as a film about a local river, need several disciplines at once. He encourages children to keep a folder of questions, build a portfolio of their own work and learn to investigate problems, and notes that a subject dropped from the timetable can still be pursued independently.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Early specialisation limits children; self-directed, cross-disciplinary projects are better preparation for an AI-shaped future, and adults should help children explore questions rather than hand them answers.
9. **Validity:** Contributor opinion essay; no research cited. AI is the backdrop rather than the main subject.

### 10. #234 HoD Space ep5: AI resource creation, exam marking, supporting science teachers

1. **Title:** #234 HoD Space ep5: AI resource creation, exam marking, supporting science teachers
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-21
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Mr Barton Maths Podcast
4. **Author(s):** Craig Barton (host); Jo Morgan (co-host)
5. **Author note:** Barton is a maths teacher and author; Morgan is a head of maths and resource creator.
6. **URL:** https://podcast.mrbartonmaths.com/234-hod-space-ep5-ai-resource-creation-exam-marking-supporting-science-teachers/
7. **Summary:**
Barton describes how he prompts AI tools to create teaching resources: setting aside the ethics of training data, investing effort in the first prompt, challenging the output and asking the tool to state its design principles. Morgan, a self-described AI sceptic, says she can spot AI involvement in many circulating resources. They test three tools on a trigonometry task and ask whether AI could match the quality of a respected task designer.

They then discuss whether exam boards should use AI marking, covering the history of automated essay marking, how such systems can be gamed, comparative judgement and why a machine error may feel harder to accept. Morgan explains why she will not trial AI marking in her department. The final segment, on maths in other subjects, is not about AI.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The hosts suggest that careful prompting and checking make AI useful for resource creation and that AI marking raises questions of accuracy, gaming and acceptance; no firm verdict is reached.
9. **Validity:** Podcast; practitioner conversation with no study cited in the AI segments. Summarised from show notes (audio not heard). The episode also promotes a paid community.

### 11. AI Misconception and Adoption Among Indonesian K-12 Teachers

1. **Title:** AI Misconception and Adoption Among Indonesian K-12 Teachers
2. **Publication date:** 2026-10-19
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** arXiv (cs.HC, cross-listed cs.CY), arXiv:2610.26758; accepted at AIED 2026
4. **Author(s):** Alham Fikri Aji, Afifa Amriani, Rendi Chevi, Ayu Purwarianti, Derry Wijaya
5. **Author note:** The authors are NLP and computing researchers working on AI in Indonesia.
6. **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26758
7. **Summary:**
The paper starts from Indonesia's national plan to bring AI into K-12 schools and argues that policy readiness does not establish teacher readiness, given the country's geography and uneven infrastructure. The authors surveyed K-12 teachers on what they understand about AI, how they and their students use it, and whether they consider it acceptable in learning activities.

Responses were analysed with a semi-automated thematic analysis that grouped misconceptions and use cases. Teachers broadly accept AI in education, mainly for efficiency, while recognising risks to deep learning and academic integrity.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Strong acceptance coexists with limited technical understanding of how AI works, so teacher AI literacy is a gap that adoption policy should address.
9. **Validity:** Preprint of a conference paper accepted at AIED 2026; teacher survey with thematic analysis. Sample size and percentages were not read from the full text.
