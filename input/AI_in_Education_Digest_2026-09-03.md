### 1. NYC's new school AI ban explained: What's changing? What's still murky?

1. **Title:** NYC's new school AI ban explained: What's changing? What's still murky? (headline also rendered as "NYC's new school AI ban explained: What students and teachers need to know")
2. **Publication date:** 2 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Chalkbeat New York (nonprofit education newsroom)
4. **Author(s):** Michael Elsen-Rooney and Alex Zimmerman
5. **Author note:** Elsen-Rooney and Zimmerman are reporters for Chalkbeat New York who cover New York City public schools.
6. **URL:** https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/
7. **Summary:**
The article reports that on 2 September 2026 Mayor Zohran Mamdani, alongside schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels, announced New York City's approach to artificial intelligence and screen time in schools for the coming year, roughly eight days before the start of the school year. According to the article, the city will ban student-facing generative AI tools — such as chatbots and tutors that respond to prompts — in preschool (2-K) through eighth-grade classrooms, while allowing limited AI use in high school, and will also limit screen time for younger students. It reports that officials said they are "disabling" AI features on 38 software programs already in use in city schools but had not released the list of those products, and that the Education Department had not published formal written guidance as of that afternoon. Mamdani described the policy as the "broadest generative AI moratorium in the nation."

The article details what was known and unknown at announcement. It states the preschool-through-eighth-grade prohibition will run for at least the coming school year, with a coalition of educators, students, parents, union officials, and experts to be convened to assess it. It reports that the digital tutor Amira and the digital version of the Houghton Mifflin Harcourt "Into Reading" curriculum were cited by a mayoral adviser as products that would turn off AI components (a Houghton Mifflin Harcourt spokesperson said "Into Reading" has no student-facing AI), and that advocates were told Google's Gemini would be shut off. It describes exemptions for assistive technology tied to Individualized Education Plans or 504 Plans and for English-language learners, and for required progress-monitoring assessments; screen-time recommendations of no individual devices for preschool through second grade, up to 30 minutes daily in grades 3–5, and up to 45 minutes in middle school; five approved high-school generative-AI pilot programs (Quill, Edia, Brisk Teaching, Playlab, and Intel AI-Ready Schools) capped at about 50,000 high-school students; two required 45-minute AI-literacy modules per year for high schoolers; permitted teacher uses (lesson planning, translation, drafting communications) and prohibited teacher uses (grading, behavior monitoring, developing special-education plans, or student mental-health support); and the absence of a spelled-out enforcement mechanism.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, New York City is imposing a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from preschool through eighth grade — described by the mayor as the broadest such moratorium in the nation — paired with screen-time limits for younger grades, limited and pilot-based high-school use, required high-school AI-literacy modules, and defined permitted and prohibited teacher uses. The article emphasizes that key implementation details, including the specific products affected and enforcement, remained unresolved days before school began. (This event was reported the same day by multiple national outlets, including The Washington Post, CNN, and NY1, and announced in a New York City Mayor's Office press release; a companion Chalkbeat news report by Amy Zimmer and Alex Zimmerman, "NYC to ban AI tools for 2-K through 8th grade, limit classroom screen time under new guidance," carries the same date. These are treated as coverage of a single event and consolidated in this entry.)

### 2. States urge schools to use AI, but leave teachers on their own to stop cheating

1. **Title:** States urge schools to use AI, but leave teachers on their own to stop cheating (also rendered as "As students use AI to cheat, state guidelines offer teachers little help")
2. **Publication date:** 2 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Chalkbeat (national desk; nonprofit education newsroom)
4. **Author(s):** Lily Altavena
5. **Author note:** Altavena is a national reporter at Chalkbeat covering K–12 education, including AI in schools and the teaching profession.
6. **URL:** https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on a Chalkbeat review of state AI guidance for public schools, finding that while roughly three out of four states (the District of Columbia and at least 37 states) have published recommendations on AI use in education, these documents tend to devote most of their attention to AI literacy and teacher efficiency and to say comparatively little about academic integrity, offering few concrete steps to deter or detect student cheating. It states that at least 13 states have passed laws on AI in education, but that those laws most often direct state agencies and districts to create their own guidance. The article cites a Common Sense Media survey published the prior month reporting that 70% of teens use AI for schoolwork, and quotes teachers and researchers, including MIT professor Justin Reich, describing the lack of evidence-based policy and the difficulty of defining and detecting AI-enabled cheating.

The article reports that more than a third of states urge schools not to rely solely on AI-detection tools, citing studies that such tools are ineffective and can misflag genuine student writing, but that many states then offer little beyond acceptable-use policies. It describes teachers responding by shifting to in-class, handwritten graded work, and includes a school librarian's argument for rethinking assessment (for example, oral assignments or a focus on the learning process). It also recounts a Manassas City (Virginia) school board's late-July rejection of a proposed policy to add AI misuse to the student code of conduct, and notes that most of the nation's ten largest districts are developing more specific anti-cheating policies, referencing New York City's newly announced policy and the fact that half of those large districts explicitly discourage AI-detection tools.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, the central finding is that most state AI guidance for schools promotes AI adoption and literacy while largely avoiding concrete direction on academic integrity, leaving teachers and districts to devise their own approaches to deterring and detecting AI-enabled cheating amid limited evidence on what works. The article frames academic integrity as a leading, and largely unaddressed, teacher concern in current state and district AI policy.

### 3. Voters express broad concerns about AI and tech in schools, survey shows

1. **Title:** Voters express broad concerns about AI and tech in schools, survey shows
2. **Publication date:** 2 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** K-12 Dive (Informa TechTarget; K–12 education industry news outlet), reporting on a survey from The Century Foundation
4. **Author(s):** Ed Finkel
5. **Author note:** Finkel is a contributing journalist for K-12 Dive who covers K–12 education policy and technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.k12dive.com/news/voters-express-broad-concerns-about-ai-and-tech-in-schools-survey-shows/829351/
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on survey data released by The Century Foundation (in a report titled "Americans Are United Against the Tech Takeover of Public Schools") indicating that a large majority of Americans, across political affiliations, want the government to clearly regulate the use of AI and other new technology in K–12 schools. It states that at least 80% of respondents were "very" or "somewhat" concerned about six technology-related issues, including students using AI to complete assignments without learning the material (85%), teachers being pressured to use AI tools without proven pedagogical benefit (81%), and schools not doing enough to prepare students for a world changing due to AI (80%). The survey, of more than 2,000 voters, was conducted in May by Morning Consult; the article notes additional concerns about social media (86%), private companies collecting and profiting from student data (84%), and cell phone use in class (81%).

The article quotes Julie Margetta Morgan, president of The Century Foundation and a report co-author, characterizing the concerns as broad and "non-ideological," and reporting that 77% of respondents (including 80% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats) said the government should set clear guardrails rather than leaving technology decisions to teachers, while 49% said in-class technology and AI use should be "kept to a minimum." Morgan is quoted arguing that district and school leaders currently bear the burden of setting rules in the absence of state action, that they should engage parents and raise the issue with lawmakers, and that federal and state legislators could "level the playing field" between technology companies and school districts on matters such as contract terms and student data.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, the survey's central claim is that American voters across the political spectrum hold broad, bipartisan concerns about AI and technology in K–12 schools and favor clear government guardrails over leaving technology decisions to individual teachers, with substantial shares also wanting classroom technology use minimized. The report frames these findings as relevant to school leaders currently setting technology policy without much state or federal direction.

### 4. Bringing ChatGPT for Teachers to more U.S. school districts

1. **Title:** Bringing ChatGPT for Teachers to more U.S. school districts
2. **Publication date:** 26 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** OpenAI (company announcement / news post)
4. **Author(s):** OpenAI (corporate authorship; includes a quotation from Leah Belsky, Vice President of Education, OpenAI)
5. **Author note:** OpenAI is the developer of ChatGPT; the post concerns its ChatGPT for Teachers product for U.S. K–12 educators.
6. **URL:** https://openai.com/index/bringing-chatgpt-for-teachers-to-more-us-school-districts/
7. **Summary:**
The announcement states that OpenAI is expanding ChatGPT for Teachers to 55 additional U.S. school systems across 20 states, bringing the product to more than 100,000 additional educators and staff, and that OpenAI is now working with more than 100 K–12 organizations across 30 states to provide free access and training to over 300,000 educators and staff. It reports that the new cohort includes 1 in 5 of America's 20 largest public school districts, and that ChatGPT for Teachers remains free for verified U.S. K–12 educators through June 2028 and is limited to administrators, faculty, and educators. The post announces a data privacy agreement described as spanning 16 states — a "National Data Privacy Agreement" via the Student Data Privacy Consortium framework — intended to give districts in participating states a common path to evaluate the product against student-data-privacy requirements, and lists signed General Offers covering 15 states plus a separate California agreement.

The announcement describes a "shared implementation model" in which OpenAI provides hands-on training, support for district leaders, and peer learning across systems, and includes quotations from district officials (in Sarasota County, Lynwood Unified, Houston ISD, Wichita Public Schools, and Township 211) about responsible adoption, privacy, and keeping educators "in control." It cites a privacy-preserving OpenAI analysis (1 January–16 July) reporting more than 1.9 million educator messages related to time-saving tasks — including 900,000 about report cards and progress reports and 800,000 about lesson planning — and references the K-12 Educator plugin, OpenAI Academy training, AI Skills Jams held with the Walton Family Foundation, and the National Academy for AI Instruction with the American Federation of Teachers. It lists the newly added district partners. (This summary reflects the company's own description of the product and program; the digest does not independently verify the product's features, figures, or privacy claims.)

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As stated by OpenAI, the announcement's central claim is that ChatGPT for Teachers is scaling to more U.S. school districts alongside new multi-state data-privacy agreements and a shared implementation model, positioning the free educator product as a governance- and privacy-conscious tool for K–12 systems. OpenAI frames its guiding position as "AI should support learning, not shortcut it," with educators remaining in control of how AI is used in classrooms.
