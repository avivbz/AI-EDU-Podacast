### 1. Michigan adds statewide AI guidance for K-12 schools

1. **Title:** Michigan adds statewide AI guidance for K-12 schools
2. **Publication date:** 14 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Michigan Public (NPR member station; Education desk)
4. **Author(s):** Hanna Donovan
5. **Author note:** Donovan is a newsroom intern at Michigan Public and a student at Grand Valley State University who reports on education topics.
6. **URL:** https://www.michiganpublic.org/education/2026-07-14/michigan-adds-statewide-ai-guidance-for-k-12-schools
7. **Summary:**
The report states that Michigan's Department of Education has partnered with an education nonprofit, Michigan Virtual, to craft and promote statewide guidance for integrating artificial intelligence with instruction for K-12 students. According to the department, the framework is intended to help students and educators navigate AI in the classroom as its use becomes more prevalent, and it outlines what students should know about using AI, including background on algorithms, data, and computational thinking. The department describes the guide as supporting "safe, equitable, and responsible AI practices" and says it is meant to ensure AI becomes part of a district's long-term planning.

The article quotes Education Department spokesperson Ken Coleman, who says it is critical that K-12 students are taught AI skills and resources so they know how to use them safely, and who notes that the department expects the guide will later include learning plans tailored to various age ranges. For now, the report states, the guide offers advice for how educators can teach AI concepts and use AI for teaching and learning, and it provides discussion prompts for local planning within school districts.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that Michigan has issued a statewide, comprehensive AI guidance framework for districts, developed with Michigan Virtual, that addresses both instruction about AI (algorithms, data, computational thinking) and the use of AI in teaching, and that the state frames the guidance as promoting safe, equitable, and responsible practices to be incorporated into districts' long-term planning.

### 2. How My School Used Common Sense and Collaboration to Confront AI

1. **Title:** How My School Used Common Sense and Collaboration to Confront AI
2. **Publication date:** 15 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** EdSurge (education-technology news site; "Voices of Change" series)
4. **Author(s):** Pattie Morales
5. **Author note:** Morales is the instructional technology coordinator at the Indian Community School, an Indigenous K-12 school, where she leads AI policy and educational-technology work.
6. **URL:** https://www.edsurge.com/news/how-my-school-used-common-sense-and-collaboration-to-confront-ai
7. **Summary:**
The first-person account describes how the author, as a one-person instructional technology department at an Indigenous school, encountered a "policy vacuum" as students began using generative AI to complete assignments before the school had either an AI policy or an academic-honesty policy in place. Morales recounts teachers praising student writing that turned out to be AI-generated, a situation she connects to what Micah Miner calls "unproductive success," and describes the difficulty of responding to perceived cheating without shared definitions, guidance, or training. She frames the school-board–level challenge as balancing pressure toward AI integration with the need to protect Indigenous data sovereignty and to reflect the varied perspectives of students and families in the Native community.

The article then details the collaborative process the author used to build a framework, including drawing on the International Society for Transforming Education's AI Explorations course and convening a cross-functional "Future Ready Team" of teachers, IT staff, and administrators. Morales says she authored layers of a framework using resources from the U.S. Department of Education, UNESCO, the European Union publications office, the Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction, TeachAI's AI Guidance for Schools Toolkit, and North Carolina's "stoplight" model, and that the school paired policy documents with staff professional development, conference learning, and an AI literacy night for families. She reports that these steps shifted teachers from "policing technology to guiding its purposeful use" and turned family anxiety into partnership.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The author's central claim is that AI "common sense" cannot be mandated or printed from national frameworks alone but must be built intentionally through research, reflection, collaboration, professional development, and community engagement. She argues that policies establish expectations but that training, time, funding, and relationships are what translate policy into practice, and she urges schools to move past compliance toward shared understanding grounded in their communities.

### 3. AI Meets ESL, and Teachers Are Intrigued

1. **Title:** AI Meets ESL, and Teachers Are Intrigued
2. **Publication date:** 16 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** EdSurge (education-technology news site)
4. **Author(s):** Adam Stone
5. **Author note:** Stone is a journalist who covers education technology, including AI in K-12 settings, for EdSurge.
6. **URL:** https://www.edsurge.com/news/ai-meets-esl-and-teachers-are-intrigued
7. **Summary:**
The article surveys how teachers and researchers view AI chatbots as tools for supporting K-12 emergent bilingual learners. It quotes Daniel Gomez, a bilingual teacher at the Ideal School in Tampa, Florida, on AI's ability to address topics across subjects and languages, and Harvard assistant professor Ying Xu on opportunities to adapt learning materials into different languages and to provide scaffolding in a child's home language. Additional practitioners—Krystle Salas of Second Mile, Melissa Henning of The Source for Learning, and educator Stephanie Howell—describe uses such as simplifying content, building student confidence to practice a new language, offering round-the-clock practice, and serving as a "guide on the side" while preserving productive struggle.

The piece also outlines family-engagement uses, including chatbots that translate simultaneously so parents who speak a home language can discuss schoolwork, and bots that send families nightly questions tied to the day's lesson. It then presents cautions raised by the same experts: AI systems are often less accurate at recognizing bilingual children's accented or code-switched speech, which could affect both learning support and assessment; the digital divide may widen gaps if students lack devices or home internet; and models trained on academic language may need customization. Several sources emphasize that AI should supplement rather than replace human instruction.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that educators and researchers see significant potential for AI chatbots to support bilingual learners and engage their families, while consistently cautioning that the tools must be used thoughtfully to avoid widening existing gaps—citing speech-recognition inaccuracies, the digital divide, and the need for customization—and that human instruction and judgment should remain primary.

### 4. A Step-by-Step Guide to AI in Schools — How Much to Use and When

1. **Title:** A Step-by-Step Guide to AI in Schools — How Much to Use and When
2. **Publication date:** 16 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The 74 (nonprofit education news outlet; Opinion / commentary)
4. **Author(s):** Jennifer Weber
5. **Author note:** Weber is a K-12 education policy fellow at the Manhattan Institute and the author of a forthcoming brief, "A Developmental Framework for AI in K-12 Education for New York State."
6. **URL:** https://www.the74million.org/article/a-step-by-step-guide-to-how-much-ai-to-use-in-schools-and-when/
7. **Summary:**
The commentary argues that, unlike earlier classroom technologies, generative AI can perform the very thinking that lessons are designed to develop, and that more than 30 states have issued AI guidance focused on data privacy, human oversight, and teacher training rather than on whether a given use is developmentally appropriate. Invoking B.F. Skinner's 1958 teaching machine and the concept of "cognitive offloading," the author cites a randomized study of roughly 1,000 high school math students in which students using a chatbot that gave direct answers performed worse on later exams than non-users, while those using a hints-only chatbot performed comparably to the no-AI group.

Weber proposes a grade-banded framework in which AI's role expands only as a child's own foundation grows. In grades K-2, she writes, AI should remain in the teacher's hands (for example, planning phonics instruction) because students are building foundational literacy and number skills through productive struggle. In grades 3-5, students should use AI only after completing work themselves and under teacher direction, such as critiquing AI-generated versions of their writing. By middle school, she argues, AI should be studied as an object of AI literacy—students should be able to explain how the systems work and fail—and by high school, students who can already read, write, and reason independently can use AI for drafting feedback and tutoring. She adds that assessments should remain AI-free across all grades.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The author's central claim is that schools should adopt a developmental framework that calibrates AI use to students' age and foundational skills—keeping AI in teachers' hands in the earliest grades, introducing student use only after independent work in upper elementary, teaching AI literacy in middle school, and permitting broader use in high school—while keeping assessments AI-free. She argues that AI's value should be measured by what students can do as a result of it, not by what the technology itself can do.

### 5. Curios of the week #176

1. **Title:** Curios of the week #176
2. **Publication date:** 17 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Filling The Pail (Substack blog/newsletter)
4. **Author(s):** Greg Ashman
5. **Author note:** Ashman is an educator and author who writes the "Filling The Pail" blog from an explicit-instruction and cognitive-load-theory perspective and evaluates education technology against learning-science evidence.
6. **URL:** https://fillingthepail.substack.com/p/curios-of-the-week-176
7. **Summary:**
The post is a weekly roundup ("Curios") of education items, opening with personal notes before turning to its "AI fail of the week." In that section, Ashman summarizes reporting by Jill Barshay in The Hechinger Report on a randomized field experiment by Alp Sungu, Benjamin Lira, and Angela Duckworth, which found that providing teachers with AI tools reduced student intrinsic motivation, as measured by surveys of interest and enjoyment. He highlights the finding that the effect was especially pronounced among students whose teachers were already weaker instructors (as measured by prior performance), whose students also scored lower on standardized final exams, and he notes that basing teacher performance on past data makes the result more robust than classroom observation.

Ashman relates the study to his own practice, stating that he believes AI can "supercharge" what good instructional designers already do and that he uses AI to create diagrams and resources while retaining quality control, typically in a strategic, non-time-pressured way. He distinguishes between AI-generated content and human-developed content iterated over time, connects the study's speculation (that motivation drops when teachers use generic rather than personally prepared resources) to schools that use common lesson plans, and argues such schools need mechanisms to ensure teachers still prepare lessons properly. A subsequent "means-ends analysis" section critiques edtech advocates associated with hype around Alpha School, including a claim that AI will teach to students' "learning styles," which he notes has long been debunked.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claims are that access to AI tools alone does not improve teaching and may reduce student motivation and, for weaker teachers, achievement; that the difference lies in whether teachers exercise judgment and quality control over AI output rather than using generic material as is; and that schools relying on common or AI-generated lesson plans need mechanisms to ensure teachers still prepare thoroughly. Ashman also reiterates skepticism toward edtech claims such as AI-driven "learning styles."

### 6. Schools as Energy Centers for Assembling Tomorrow

1. **Title:** Schools as Energy Centers for Assembling Tomorrow
2. **Publication date:** 19 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Disrupted: Teaching and Learning in An AI World (Substack)
4. **Author(s):** Stefan Bauschard
5. **Author note:** Bauschard is an education author and consultant focused on the implications of AI for teaching, learning, and assessment, and writes the "Education Disrupted" newsletter for educators and school leaders.
6. **URL:** https://stefanbauschard.substack.com/p/schools-as-energy-centers-for-assembling
7. **Summary:**
The essay, addressed to education leaders, argues that the United States has an "allocation problem" rather than a human-energy shortage, contending that schools spend too much leadership effort on detecting, monitoring, and prohibiting AI use rather than on enabling students and teachers to build. Bauschard criticizes investments in AI-text detection software (citing OpenAI's 2023 shutdown of its own classifier and Vanderbilt's disabling of Turnitin's AI detector) and student-monitoring software, framing them as signals of distrust that dissipate student and teacher energy. He extends this critique to legislative and district AI moratoriums, characterizing them as attempts to "freeze the capability instead of governing it," and argues that a general-purpose technology cannot be controlled from a district office.

The post contrasts "school as energy center" with "school as data center," calling on leaders to redirect resources toward empowerment: cancelling detection subscriptions to fund teacher time, replacing "integrity-by-surveillance" with "integrity-by-standing" (demonstrations, oral defenses, and performances), rewriting district AI guidance as an invitation that includes teachers and students on design teams, and measuring leadership by "energy directed" (projects shipped, experiments run, students who started something unassigned). Bauschard frames educators as uniquely positioned assets in a low-trust moment, cites Gallup data on K-12 burnout and workplace disrespect, and includes a conditioning caveat that empowerment should not become a top-down mandate to adopt AI, with skeptics welcomed onto design teams.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claim is that school leaders should shift from a control-and-surveillance posture toward one that "unleashes" the existing energy of students and teachers, treating schools as sites for building the future ("assembling tomorrow") rather than for containing or optimizing students. Bauschard argues this transformation requires no new schools, campuses, or funding—only a redirection of leadership priorities—and he offers concrete, budget-neutral steps while cautioning that AI adoption should be an invitation rather than a mandate.

### 7. AI embraced by more students and educators, Instructure finds

1. **Title:** AI embraced by more students and educators, Instructure finds
2. **Publication date:** 22 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** K-12 Dive (K-12 education industry news; "Dive Brief")
4. **Author(s):** Anna Merod
5. **Author note:** Merod is a reporter at K-12 Dive who covers K-12 education policy and technology, including AI.
6. **URL:** https://www.k12dive.com/news/ai-embraced-by-more-students-and-educators-instructure-finds/825826/
7. **Summary:**
The brief reports on a survey released by the ed-tech company Instructure (owner of the Canvas learning management system) finding that AI use is becoming commonplace in K-12 schools: 68% of educators reported using it at least occasionally, and 73% of parents said the same of their children. According to the article, both groups expressed concerns about AI's impact on learning while also finding reasons for optimism—parents most often citing improved accessibility for diverse learning needs and educators most often citing time savings. The survey found that 33% of educators and 30% of parents favored restricting AI use, and that respondents ranked teaching responsible AI use and developing AI literacy as their highest priorities for schools. Instructure's sample of 1,125 people, surveyed June 12-15, included K-12 and higher-education educators, current or recent higher-education students, and parents of K-12 students.

The article situates the findings against a backdrop of continued calls for AI moratoriums, noting that the coalition Schools Beyond Screens called for an AI moratorium in all districts a day after Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute released a risk assessment of Google's AI Overview and AI Mode. It reports additional survey findings on parental concerns (academic integrity and plagiarism at 62%, loss of critical-thinking and deep-learning skills at 61%, and overreliance at 59%), that both parents and educators found AI more acceptable for tasks like explaining concepts or brainstorming than for determining grades, and that 45% of educators reported no AI training. Instructure recommended that leaders teach AI literacy and responsible use, provide ongoing professional learning, set boundaries, and build evidence of AI's impacts over time.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports Instructure's findings that AI adoption among K-12 students and educators has grown and that most educators and parents are not in favor of restrictions, even as a substantial minority support them and concerns about integrity, critical thinking, and overreliance persist. It relays Instructure's recommendation that schools respond by prioritizing AI literacy, responsible-use instruction, professional development, and clear boundaries rather than simple restriction, and situates the data amid ongoing moratorium efforts.
