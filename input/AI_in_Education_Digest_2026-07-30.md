### 1. This New York superintendent is embracing a human-first approach to AI in schools

1. **Title:** This New York superintendent is embracing a human-first approach to AI in schools
2. **Publication date:** 30 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** K-12 Dive (K-12 education industry news; "Lessons in Leadership" column)
4. **Author(s):** Roger Riddell
5. **Author note:** Riddell is a senior editor at K-12 Dive who covers K-12 education technology, leadership, and policy.
6. **URL:** https://www.k12dive.com/news/this-new-york-superintendent-is-embracing-a-human-first-approach-to-ai-in-s/826531/
7. **Summary:**
The article is an edited interview with Jared Bloom, superintendent of the Franklin Square Union Free School District, a 1,900-student pre-K–6 district in New York, as part of K-12 Dive's "Lessons in Leadership" series. Bloom, a former internet-industry worker who entered teaching in 2000 and later moved into administration, describes his district's approach to integrating AI while keeping "humans and core skills first." He outlines two main uses: teachers using AI to make their work more efficient and to differentiate instruction more quickly, and tools that use AI to support classroom learning within a "walled-off garden" that teachers control and monitor. He gives an example of a library lesson in which students held "conversations" with historical figures and, when different students received different answers, were prompted to consult primary sources to verify information. He states repeatedly that AI "does not replace great teachers ever" and emphasizes that no personally identifiable information is entered into any AI tool the district uses.

The interview also addresses parental and community concerns. Bloom says the district has been transparent from the start, discussing AI in newsletters, parent workshops, and "coffee with the superintendent" sessions, and frames exposure to AI as necessary so students learn to use it appropriately rather than encountering it only outside school (drawing a comparison to the social media era). On educator training, he describes a director of technology innovation, a full-time technology coach, and building-level "champions," along with in-service courses and professional development offered during and after the school day, some mandatory and some optional. He recounts that AI can compress tasks such as producing leveled reading passages from about a week to 10–20 minutes, while reiterating that teachers remain the experts who know students best.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The piece presents Bloom's position that AI should be integrated into K-12 instruction as a tool that supports, rather than replaces, teachers, with data privacy as the top priority, teacher control over student-facing tools, transparency with families, and sustained professional development. He argues that schools cannot ignore technological change, that students must learn to use AI responsibly and verify its outputs, and that technology is worth adopting when it helps teachers do their jobs better and use data to drive instruction.

### 2. 'AI is here to stay': Columbus County Schools launches classroom AI platform

1. **Title:** 'AI is here to stay': Columbus County Schools launches classroom AI platform
2. **Publication date:** 28 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** WECT (Gray Media NBC affiliate, Wilmington, North Carolina; Education desk)
4. **Author(s):** Reyna Crooms
5. **Author note:** Crooms is a WECT news reporter covering local news in southeastern North Carolina, including K-12 education.
6. **URL:** https://www.wect.com/2026/07/28/ai-is-here-stay-columbus-county-schools-launches-classroom-ai-platform/
7. **Summary:**
The report states that Columbus County Schools in North Carolina will incorporate an AI platform called Magic School for the first time during the upcoming school year. According to district leaders, Magic School is an education-focused AI platform built with school-approved tools rather than open-access chatbots, and is described as a "thought partner" designed to walk students through a thought process and support tasks such as brainstorming and writing rather than generating quick answers. Laura Hunter, the district's Chief Academic Officer, says the district saw a significant uptick in AI use among students and teachers over the previous school year and that it is the district's job to teach students to use AI responsibly and to critically evaluate what AI generates.

The article reports that Magic School is designed to follow both the district's curriculum standards and state standards, and that the district can create and control chatbots shaped by its own learning framework and curriculum documents. Hunter says the platform will be used in middle and high school classrooms with a slow rollout, that teachers will also use it for lesson planning, and that the district wanted to "lean into the idea that AI could help teachers be more effective and more efficient." The report notes that Columbus County Schools paid $29,950.61 for Magic School this year and that students will no longer have access to other AI platforms while on school networks.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that the district is adopting a controlled, curriculum-aligned AI platform for middle and high school use and for teacher lesson planning, framing the move as a response to rising student and teacher AI use and as a way to teach responsible, critical use rather than to ban the technology. District leadership's central claim, as quoted, is that AI "is here to stay" and that schools have a responsibility to teach students to use emerging technology safely and responsibly.

### 3. As AI seeps into classrooms, Charleston school district prepares teachers for what's next

1. **Title:** As AI seeps into classrooms, Charleston school district prepares teachers for what's next
2. **Publication date:** 21 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The Post and Courier (Charleston, South Carolina; "Education Lab")
4. **Author(s):** Valerie Nava
5. **Author note:** Nava is an education reporter with The Post and Courier's Education Lab in Charleston, covering K-12 schools in South Carolina.
6. **URL:** https://www.postandcourier.com/education-lab/charleston-county-schools-ai-classrooms/article_4e83cd49-5fac-4577-9f52-951dd6e47cea.html
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on the Charleston County School District's efforts to prepare educators and students to use AI in classrooms during the 2026-27 academic year. According to the report, the district devoted more than a dozen sessions at its multi-day annual professional development event to AI literacy and classroom application, training teachers who are expected to help bring practices to their own classrooms. The reporting describes educators demonstrating how AI can be used to adapt lessons and help students access content regardless of language proficiency or particular learning needs, citing a teacher using AI to support students with different learning needs.

The piece situates the training within the district's broader, previously adopted AI policy and its stated intention to prepare both educators and students for classrooms and a workforce in which AI is common. Rather than banning AI, the district is described as investing in teacher training under defined guardrails ahead of the coming school year, when AI literacy instruction is expected to reach teachers and students across the district.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that Charleston County School District is responding to growing AI use by investing in extensive teacher professional development and AI literacy ahead of the 2026-27 school year, taking an approach centered on preparation and guardrails rather than prohibition. (Note: portions of the article are behind a subscription paywall; this summary is based on the accessible reporting and corroborating coverage, and details beyond those could not be independently verified.)

### 4. July 24 Update: Where is Education on AGI? AI starts to collapse human math; Kimi for the world; Rapid model advances and more coming; new models and jobs

1. **Title:** July 24 Update: Where is Education on AGI? AI starts to collapse human math; Kimi for the world; Rapid model advances and more coming; new models and jobs
2. **Publication date:** 23 July 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Disrupted: Teaching and Learning in An AI World (Substack)
4. **Author(s):** Stefan Bauschard
5. **Author note:** Bauschard is an education author and consultant who writes the "Education Disrupted" newsletter on the implications of AI for teaching, learning, and assessment.
6. **URL:** https://stefanbauschard.substack.com/p/july-24-update-where-is-education
7. **Summary:**
The post is a wide-ranging periodic update whose overarching argument is framed for educators: it juxtaposes rapid advances in AI capability (which the author documents at length, including AI systems reportedly achieving perfect scores on the 2026 International Mathematical Olympiad, a claimed counterexample to an 87-year-old conjecture, new frontier and open-weight model releases, and reported instances of recursive self-improvement) with what the author characterizes as the current focus of K-12 school debates. Bauschard contends that "the battle over AI in schools has largely become a backlash against devices and the use of AI to teach," while, in his view, the more consequential question is how to prepare students for a world in which advanced AI can perform much of the knowledge work schools currently train students to do. He cites the OSTP report "Science: A New Golden Age" (published 21 July) and its concept of "process knowledge" to argue that some expertise is tacit and transmitted through apprenticeship rather than documents.

Applying this to schooling, Bauschard argues that both the "enthusiast" and "abstinence" camps are, in his framing, arguing about tool access rather than about what AI makes scarce. He criticizes district responses such as multi-year moratoriums (referencing New York City) and AI-detection tools as inadequate to the underlying challenge, and argues that the necessary response is "instructional redesign," using debate and other coached, live, "answerable" forms of assessment as examples of apprenticeship-style learning he says cannot be replicated by AI or by produced-alone essays. The update also contains extensive sections on mathematics, model releases, and labor-market developments that are not specific to K-12.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central education claim is that K-12 schools are, in the author's view, focused on the wrong question—whether students may use AI tools—rather than on redesigning teaching, learning, and assessment to prepare students for a world of highly capable AI. Bauschard argues that policy measures (honor codes, AI-use rubrics) and technical measures (lockdown browsers, detectors) do not address what he sees as the core variable—what students are asked to do and how they demonstrate they did it—and he advocates instructional redesign toward live, coached, verifiable performance.
