### 1. Students from All 50 States Produce National Framework for AI in America's Schools

1. **Title:** Students from All 50 States Produce National Framework for AI in America's Schools
2. **Publication date:** 3 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** AASA, The School Superintendents Association (news release; organized jointly with Day of AI, MIT RAISE, and the Edward M. Kennedy Institute for the U.S. Senate)
4. **Author(s):** AASA (institutional news release; media contacts Dan Rea of Day of AI, Lara Wade of AASA, and Olivia Wong of the Edward M. Kennedy Institute)
5. **Author note:** AASA is the national professional association for U.S. school-system superintendents; the release reports on a student policy exercise convened with MIT RAISE (an MIT Media Lab AI-education initiative) and Day of AI (a K–12 AI-literacy nonprofit founded through MIT).
6. **URL:** https://www.aasa.org/news-media/news/2026/08/03/students-produce-national-framework-for-ai-in-america's-schools
7. **Summary:**
The release announces the "STUDENTS FIRST Act of 2026," a model framework for the use of AI in K–12 education drafted by students representing all 50 states at America's Youth AI Festival, held 17–19 July 2026 on the UMass Boston and MIT campuses. Selected students formed a "National AI Student Senate" that replicated Senate procedure inside a full-scale replica of the Senate chamber at the Edward M. Kennedy Institute, dividing into subcommittees to draft, debate, and vote on the bill, which passed 82–16. The release states that the framework rejects both an outright ban on AI and its unrestricted adoption, and instead sets boundaries for when AI may support learning and when original student work, professional judgment, and human connection must remain paramount.

The release itemizes the framework's provisions. These include requiring AI-literacy instruction before or when students first use digital devices; prohibiting students at all grade levels from using AI to generate written or artistic assignments; permitting, from 9th grade, teacher-approved AI use for brainstorming, studying, and editing with disclosure; prohibiting AI use on graded assessments and allowing teachers to require oral defenses, handwritten assessments, or in-person discussions to confirm understanding; giving students a right to appeal accusations of improper AI use with human review; barring schools from relying solely on AI to set grades, determine discipline, profile students, or make hiring decisions; establishing data-privacy and transparency requirements; permitting students and educators to decline AI use with alternatives; and prohibiting AI-generated media used to bully, harass, or defame members of the school community. The release states that AASA, Day of AI, MIT RAISE, and the Kennedy Institute will circulate the framework through webinars, workshops, and school visits, and that AASA will share it with its network of more than 10,000 school leaders.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The release presents the student-authored framework as an opening contribution to a national conversation on AI in schools, intended to be evaluated, tested, and refined rather than adopted as final. As quoted, the organizers state that students are asking neither for schools to ignore AI nor to adopt it without limits, but for clear, fair rules that protect student privacy, preserve the ability to think, and ensure that AI supports human relationships in education.

### 2. An alphabet without an F: AI slop seeps into Teachers Pay Teachers curriculum marketplace

1. **Title:** An alphabet without an F: AI slop seeps into Teachers Pay Teachers curriculum marketplace (headline also rendered as "Education's version of Etsy may have an AI slop problem")
2. **Publication date:** 3 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Chalkbeat (nonprofit education newsroom; "AI in Education" section)
4. **Author(s):** Lily Altavena
5. **Author note:** Altavena is a national reporter at Chalkbeat covering curriculum, instruction, and technology in K–12 education.
6. **URL:** https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/08/03/ai-slop-on-teachers-curriculum-marketplace/
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on the appearance of what Chalkbeat describes as low-quality, AI-generated material ("AI slop") on Teachers Pay Teachers (rebranded TPT), an online marketplace where educators buy and sell lesson plans, worksheets, and classroom materials that the article says is used by 85% of U.S. pre-K–12 educators. Chalkbeat reviewed items for sale that contained obvious errors and incongruous images, including a poster on Black inventors that mislabels an image, a for-sale alphabet worksheet missing the letter F, and a U.S. presidents poster with a bizarre illustration. The piece quotes Paul Mishkin, CEO of TPT parent company IXL Learning, who states that the company uses algorithmic tools to identify and demote stores associated with low-quality AI-generated content and that such products account for a very small fraction of sales.

The article situates the phenomenon within longstanding debate over TPT's quality and within the pressure teachers face to produce instructional materials, quoting Elon University education professor Jeff Carpenter on why time-pressed teachers turn to such sites, and Northern Arizona University professor Catharyn Shelton on how a marketplace model can incentivize volume over quality. It notes markers teachers use to spot AI-generated content and reports that Chalkbeat contacted several vendors whose materials appeared AI-generated; some acknowledged using "AI-assisted tools" as part of their process while stating they review materials before publishing. The article closes by noting that a reporter prompted ChatGPT to answer such inquiries in the voice of a resource creator, and the model produced similar statements about using AI-assisted tools and reviewing content before publication.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that apparently AI-generated, error-containing materials are being sold to teachers on a widely used curriculum marketplace and could reach classrooms, and that the platform's operator says automated measures keep such content to a minuscule share of sales. It presents the concern, attributed to educators and academics quoted, that the volume of low-quality AI content compounds an existing problem of teachers lacking time and vetted, high-quality instructional materials.

### 3. Teachers Forge Ahead on Integrating AI

1. **Title:** Teachers Forge Ahead on Integrating AI
2. **Publication date:** 4 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** EdSurge (education-technology news; a service of ISTE+ASCD)
4. **Author(s):** Ed Finkel
5. **Author note:** Finkel is a freelance education journalist who contributes reporting on K–12 and education technology to EdSurge.
6. **URL:** https://www.edsurge.com/news/teachers-forge-ahead-on-integrating-ai
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on findings from Turnitin's "Learning Integrity Insights Report Q2," based on data gathered in the first half of 2026 and on analysis of student use of the Turnitin Clarity writing-transparency tool. It states that 48% of respondents said teachers and administrators take primary responsibility for AI implementation at their schools and districts, compared with technology leaders (17%) and cross-functional committees (16%). Educators interviewed relate the findings to their own settings: Matt Moyer, an elementary principal in Pottstown, Pennsylvania, describes schools deciding case by case what to implement; Antero Garcia, president of the National Council of Teachers of English, calls the teacher-led pattern "heartening" and references the NCTE's ELA AI framework; and Tim Needles of Smithtown (NY) Central School District describes administrators as more cautious than teachers and predicts more consistent AI usage within a few years.

The article summarizes further report findings and reactions. It states that educators do not view AI as worth investment unless it offers flexibility, customization, transparency into student use, and time savings or concrete instructional benefits, and it quotes Bellwether senior analyst Marisa Mission on the value of education-specific AI tools. On student use, it reports that U.S. higher-education students turned over writing to AI (submissions measured as more than 80% AI-generated) at roughly double the UK and Australian rates, while at the K–12 level only 5% to 6% of papers reached that threshold across the three countries; it also reports that students most often request AI feedback during drafting (43%), with most seeking rubric-alignment or improvement feedback, and quotes educators on students' need to learn how to ask for useful feedback. Perspectives from a New Zealand and a Mexico City educator are also included.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports the Turnitin data as indicating that teachers and administrators, rather than technology departments, are leading AI adoption in schools; that educators want AI tools built specifically for classroom use; and that students increasingly turn to AI for feedback, particularly at the drafting stage. The through-line quoted from interviewees is that teachers need education-specific AI and that students get the best outcomes when teachers control how AI is used in their classrooms.

### 4. Putting the AI Cart Before the Instructional Redesign Horse — Why "AI as Edtech" Fails

1. **Title:** Putting the AI Cart Before the Instructional Redesign Horse — Why "AI as Edtech" Fails
2. **Publication date:** 2 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Disrupted: Teaching and Learning in An AI World (Substack)
4. **Author(s):** Stefan Bauschard
5. **Author note:** Bauschard is an education author and consultant who writes the "Education Disrupted" newsletter on the implications of AI for teaching, learning, and assessment.
6. **URL:** https://stefanbauschard.substack.com/p/putting-the-ai-cart-before-the-instructional
7. **Summary:**
The post is an opinion essay framed around a conversation the author had with "Rai," a digital twin of Ray Kurzweil, which asked what it would take to get more teachers and students using AI. Bauschard offers two answers. The first is that most people, including school leaders, do not yet understand the scale of the change, and that sustained explanation is itself the core work. The second, which the essay develops, is that schools have proceeded in the wrong order: they pushed AI tools, licenses, and prompting workshops onto schools before redesigning the underlying teaching, learning, and assessment. Because most school assessment is product-based (essays, worksheets, projects that a student turns in and a teacher grades), he argues that introducing a technology whose defining trait is producing such artifacts "jammed" the existing assessment engine and alienated teachers, and that professional development compounded the problem by teaching tools before redesigning instruction.

Bauschard argues that the sequence should be reversed: instructional redesign first, using methods such as debate, project-based learning, design thinking, and games that he says decenter the teacher from the attentional center of the room while keeping the teacher central as leader, coach, and designer. Only then, he contends, should AI be integrated, positioned as a collaborator or interlocutor rather than a product-making machine, citing Ethan and Lilach Mollick's seven instructional roles for AI and Mairéad Pratschke's work on AI as a collaborator in learning. He argues that skipping redesign produces justified fears of teacher replacement, destabilizes school leaders, invites budget objections, and reduces AI to "amplifying" existing routines through vendor products; with redesign, he says, the same tools read as teaching assistants and sparring partners. The essay uses extended metaphors and asserts that the traditional lecture-and-recall model was already failing before AI made its limits harder to ignore.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claim is that AI keeps failing in schools because districts adopt the technology before redesigning instruction and assessment, and that the necessary order is instructional redesign first and AI integration second. Bauschard argues that in a "decentered," redesign-first classroom AI functions as a partner in learning rather than a threat, and that continuing to buy technology without redesigning instruction is, in his phrasing, "the most expensive way ever devised to stay exactly the same."

### 5. Temple ISD set to phase AI into classrooms and roll out new campus safety upgrades

1. **Title:** Temple ISD set to phase AI into classrooms and roll out new campus safety upgrades
2. **Publication date:** 5 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** KWTX News 10 (Gray Media CBS affiliate, Waco, Texas; "Back to School" / Education desk)
4. **Author(s):** Olivea Herzberg
5. **Author note:** Herzberg is a KWTX News 10 morning reporter covering Central Texas, including local K–12 education.
6. **URL:** https://www.kwtx.com/2026/08/05/temple-isd-set-phase-ai-into-classrooms-roll-out-new-campus-safety-upgrades/
7. **Summary:**
The report describes Temple Independent School District's plans for the 2026–27 school year, which begins 13 August, combining classroom-technology changes with campus-safety and staffing updates. On technology, it states that AI will be phased in for students during the coming year, following three years in which district staff trained with the technology to support what the district describes as safe and effective integration. The report quotes Superintendent Dr. Bobby Ott, who frames AI adoption as part of the district's responsibility to prepare students for the workforce and life after high school, states that adults must be in charge of how AI is used, and says he does not believe students should embrace it in an unfettered way.

The remainder of the report covers related district updates not specific to AI: safety measures funded by a voter-approved Tax Ratification Election (Proposition A, passed with 59% support), including new security cameras, cybersecurity upgrades, and perimeter fencing, along with a police officer stationed at every elementary school through a joint contract with the city; and staffing, including 85 new teacher hires (about a 30% drop from previous years) alongside an 84% teacher-retention rate.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The report states that Temple ISD will introduce AI to students gradually in the 2026–27 year after a multi-year staff-training period, with district leadership framing the rollout as adult-directed workforce preparation rather than unrestricted student use. The AI plan is presented as one part of a broader set of back-to-school changes spanning technology, safety, and staffing.
