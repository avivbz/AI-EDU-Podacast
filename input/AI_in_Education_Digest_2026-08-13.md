### 1. Education's AI 'gold rush' comes with risks for students

1. **Title:** Education's AI 'gold rush' comes with risks for students
2. **Publication date:** 7 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The Christian Science Monitor (Back to School special issue / cover story)
4. **Author(s):** Aaron Glantz
5. **Author note:** Glantz is a staff writer at The Christian Science Monitor reporting on education, including K–12 technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.csmonitor.com/USA/Education/2026/0807/ai-risks-schools-students
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on the rapid commercial expansion of AI products in U.S. K–12 schools and the accompanying uncertainty over which tools help or harm students. It cites a Future Market Insights analysis prepared for the Monitor stating that education's AI sector grew to $730 million in 2026 and is projected to grow 40% annually for a decade, reaching $18.5 billion in 2036, and names companies such as MagicSchool, SchoolAI, Buddy, and the startup Bloomy competing in the space. It reports that, per the Center for Democracy and Technology, an estimated 85% of teachers and 86% of students already use generative AI in schoolwork, and that frontier firms Anthropic and OpenAI are offering their products free to educators temporarily (Anthropic through June 2027, OpenAI through June 2028), which Stanford's Chris Agnew characterizes as an effort to capture the market. The piece notes the Trump administration's July 2025 Department of Education guidance encouraging AI use without setting vetting standards, and that more than 30 states have adopted their own guidelines, with Wyoming and North Carolina cited as providing more detailed frameworks.

The article presents a range of views on benefits and risks. Bloomy founder Alex Southmayd argues for personalized, gamified AI instruction and says his company aims to free teachers rather than replace them, while Agnew states that the effectiveness of "personalized learning" chatbots has not been independently established and describes a documented "performance cliff" in which students' gains disappear once AI is removed. It references a University of Pennsylvania Wharton study of 1,300 participants and 10,000 trials describing "cognitive surrender," and reports Common Sense Media's rating of the school version of Google's Gemini as "high risk," including that it treats a sixth grader the same as a high school senior. The article also recounts governance controversies, including the June 21 resignation of Los Angeles Unified Superintendent Alberto Carvalho after an FBI raid tied to an AI chatbot contract and a New York City move to pause AI software purchases amid a petition for a two-year moratorium. It closes with a Los Altos High School chemistry teacher's account of teaching "AI literacy" through source evaluation.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that spending on AI in K–12 is rising sharply amid a commercial "gold rush," while rigorous evidence of efficacy is limited and some research indicates harm to students' independent thinking. It conveys expert views that AI shows more promise for reducing teachers' routine and administrative workload than as a replacement for classroom instruction, and that, absent federal standards, responsibility for vetting tools falls to individual districts and educators.

### 2. As students head back to school, districts grapple with new AI policies

1. **Title:** As students head back to school, districts grapple with new AI policies
2. **Publication date:** 4 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Central Florida Public Media (WMFE; NPR member station), Education desk
4. **Author(s):** Kayla Kissel
5. **Author note:** Kissel is an education reporter at Central Florida Public Media covering K–12 schools in the Orlando region.
6. **URL:** https://www.cfpublic.org/education/2026-08-04/as-students-head-back-to-school-districts-grapple-with-new-ai-policies
7. **Summary:**
The report describes how several Central Florida school districts are adopting AI policies as the new school year begins, shifting from prohibition toward defining where AI fits in student learning. Orange County Public Schools approved a policy—developed with roughly 20,000 survey responses from teachers and parents plus student town halls—that lets students use AI for tasks such as background research but not to produce graded work without written teacher permission, and requires teachers to give written guidelines for AI use. The article contrasts approaches across districts: Seminole County provides district-approved AI tools and offers AI coursework at Crooms Academy of Information Technology and Hagerty High School, while Brevard County's policy requires administrator approval before AI tools are used and directs students and teachers to fact-check outputs. A Brevard board member is quoted expressing discomfort with AI and confusion about the policy, and the Brevard superintendent states students may use AI to help with research and improve a draft but not to write a paper.

The second half of the report examines cognitive and developmental concerns. Citing a 2025 Pew Research Center survey, it reports that 54% of U.S. teens have used AI chatbots for schoolwork, 57% to find information, and that one in ten teens do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help, while 59% say students at their school use AI to cheat at least sometimes. Jennifer Katzenstein, co-director of the Center for Behavioral Health at Johns Hopkins All Children's Hospital, discusses the still-developing adolescent frontal lobe and the concept of "cognitive offloading," distinguishing offloading for memory or emotional coping from offloading higher-order problem-solving and critical thinking. She states that AI is part of the future and that the priority is teaching children to use it appropriately.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The report states that Central Florida districts are moving from banning AI to setting guardrails that permit limited, teacher-directed use while prohibiting AI-produced graded work, and that policies vary by district. It presents the view, attributed to a neuropsychologist, that AI can support learning but may bypass the cognitive struggle through which core skills develop, making guidance on appropriate use important.

### 3. Deepfakes Are Supercharging Cyberbullying. How Should Schools Respond?

1. **Title:** Deepfakes Are Supercharging Cyberbullying. How Should Schools Respond?
2. **Publication date:** 6 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Week (Technology / Artificial Intelligence section)
4. **Author(s):** Olina Banerji
5. **Author note:** Banerji is a staff writer at Education Week who covers school leadership.
6. **URL:** https://www.edweek.org/technology/deepfakes-are-supercharging-cyberbullying-how-should-schools-respond/2026/08
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on how K–12 schools are responding to a rise in AI-generated deepfakes—fake, nonconsensual videos or images of students and educators—created by students, and the difficulty schools face in disciplining perpetrators while supporting victims. It notes that several states have criminalized creating and distributing nonconsensual intimate imagery and that the federal Take It Down Act, signed in May 2025, requires social media companies to remove such content within 48 hours of a verified request. Kristin Woelfel, policy counsel at the Center for Democracy and Technology, argues that punishment has outweighed prevention and victim support, and that policies should make clear both consequences and reporting pathways.

Drawing on interviews with experts and Illinois school leaders, the article lays out three recommended elements of a school response. First, tap into existing policies: several districts, and Illinois by legislation, have folded deepfakes into cyberbullying policies, with Willowbrook High School principal Daniel Krause describing added definitions and criminal-consequence language. Second, treat education as prevention: Krause has addressed AI misuse in back-to-school messaging, and Buffalo Grove High School principal Jeff Wardle emphasizes positive digital-citizenship norms tied to school values and parent-facing presentations. Third, focus on restoration: schools should have reporting protocols and a designated point person, may involve school resource officers and law enforcement, and can use restorative "reentry meetings" alongside any legal process.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports experts' and administrators' shared view that schools should respond to AI-generated deepfakes primarily through prevention, education, and restorative support for victims rather than punishment alone, and that incorporating deepfakes into existing cyberbullying policies, clarifying legal consequences, and establishing reporting and restoration protocols are the recommended steps.

### 4. AI-driven private school company assesses Kansas City for future campus

1. **Title:** AI-driven private school company assesses Kansas City for future campus (also referenced as "Metro school districts focus on AI guidelines as AI-focused school eyes possible expansion in Kansas City")
2. **Publication date:** 10 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** KCTV5 (Gray Media CBS affiliate, Kansas City), Education desk
4. **Author(s):** Jordan Fremstad
5. **Author note:** Fremstad is a KCTV5 reporter covering the Kansas City metro, including local education.
6. **URL:** https://www.kctv5.com/2026/08/10/metro-school-districts-focus-ai-guidelines-ai-focused-school-eyes-possible-expansion-kansas-city/
7. **Summary:**
The report describes how three Kansas City–area public school systems—Shawnee Mission, Olathe, and Kansas City, Kansas—are establishing AI rules as the school year begins, with a shared emphasis on preventing cheating and protecting student data, and pairs this with news that the AI-focused private-school company Alpha School is assessing the metro for a possible future campus. Shawnee Mission's board met to discuss student and teacher AI use; Superintendent Mike Schumacher is quoted saying the district wants balance between what technology can do and what may need to be "reined in," and plans to form a public work group before finalizing policy. Olathe Public Schools presented AI guidelines to its board in April, backing district-approved tools that teachers vet and monitor while blocking other open-source AI platforms over academic-integrity and data-privacy concerns; its technology director frames the plan around AI literacy and responsible use. Kansas City, Kansas, Public Schools has built a dedicated website with AI guidelines for families.

On the private-school angle, the report quotes Sofia Swanson, communications manager for Alpha School Expansion, stating that the company is gathering interest from local families and assessing demand, that it has not confirmed or announced a Kansas City location, and that such a location is "likely in the coming year." Alpha School is described as operating AI-focused campuses.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The report states that Kansas City metro districts are each building AI guidelines centered on responsible use, cheating prevention, and data protection, generally favoring vetted district-approved tools, and that an AI-centric private-school operator is separately evaluating whether to expand into the market.

### 5. New Rules for Back to School 2026: Now Comes the Hard Part

1. **Title:** New Rules for Back to School 2026: Now Comes the Hard Part
2. **Publication date:** 10 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Psychology Today (blog: "Raising Humans in a Digital World")
4. **Author(s):** Diana E. Graber, M.A.
5. **Author note:** Graber is co-founder of Cyberwise, founder of the Cyber Civics middle-school digital-literacy curriculum, and author of *Raising Humans in a Digital World*; she writes on children's relationship with technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/raising-humans-in-a-digital-world/202608/new-rules-for-back-to-school-2026-now-comes-the-hard
7. **Summary:**
The blog post argues that the new school year's rules on phones, screen time, and AI address real problems but cannot by themselves build the capacities—attention, judgment, critical thinking, relationships—that students need in an AI-infused world. On phone policy, it reports that as of June 2026, 22 states and the District of Columbia had adopted bell-to-bell cellphone bans and another 19 had enacted more flexible local-restriction laws, and cites a Pew Research Center survey (late May–early June 2026) finding 77% of U.S. adults support banning phones during class and 48% support all-day bans. On AI, it cites Common Sense Media's 2026 census of more than 1,200 children ages 9–17 finding that 86% had used generative AI and 24% used it daily, and that among users, 85% used it for schoolwork; it notes that three-quarters of students say their school has discussed permitted AI uses but just over half have been taught to use it safely.

The post develops a distinction between AI as a "scaffold" that supports student thinking and AI as a "substitute" that bypasses the planning, working memory, and argument-building through which learning occurs, referencing a 2024 study in *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence* on adolescents with greater executive-function challenges perceiving generative AI as more useful for schoolwork. It also addresses AI companions, citing a 2025 Common Sense Media survey in which 72% of teens had used an AI companion and reporting that younger teens (ages 13–14) trust AI-companion advice more than older teens, alongside a 2025 Common Sense Media–Stanford Medicine risk assessment finding leading chatbots unsafe for teen mental-health support. The post concludes that attention, thinking, trust, and relationships must be practiced and taught, work it frames as belonging to humans.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claim is that device bans and AI policies shape the school environment but do not by themselves cultivate the durable skills—focus, judgment, critical thinking, and relational capacity—that students need, and that the distinction between using AI as a scaffold versus a substitute is what matters most for learning. It argues that these capacities must be explicitly taught and practiced rather than assumed to follow from rules.

### 6. As students return to school, AI takes a seat in Charleston County classrooms

1. **Title:** As students return to school, AI takes a seat in Charleston County classrooms
2. **Publication date:** 11 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Live 5 News / WCSC (Gray Media CBS affiliate, Charleston, South Carolina), Education / Back-to-School desk
4. **Author(s):** Gracie Kirschner
5. **Author note:** Kirschner is a Live 5 News (WCSC) reporter covering the Charleston, South Carolina area, including local K–12 education.
6. **URL:** https://www.live5news.com/2026/08/11/students-return-school-ai-takes-seat-charleston-county-classrooms/
7. **Summary:**
The report describes the Charleston County School District (CCSD) launching a districtwide AI policy for the new school year that aims to teach students and staff to use AI responsibly rather than keep it out of classrooms. Superintendent Anita Huggins states CCSD is among the first districts nationally to implement a comprehensive AI approach, and Deputy Superintendent Luke Clamp frames adoption as beginning with literacy—building staff, student, and family understanding of how the technology works. Clamp describes the district's "SEE" framework (safe, ethical, effective): considering whether a tool protects data and produces sound output, whether AI is used to enhance rather than replace critical thinking and original ideas, and whether AI-generated information has been analyzed for accuracy before use. The report notes that during the summer, Charleston County teachers took professional development with the AI for Education Foundation on how generative AI works and how it can support instruction, lesson planning, and assignments while protecting academic integrity.

The report states the policy provides guidance rather than requirements, leaving teachers to decide whether and how to use AI tools, and that every student from early grades through high school will receive some AI-literacy instruction this year. Clamp encourages parents to stay involved in how their children use technology, and both Clamp and Huggins emphasize that the district does not view AI as a replacement for teachers or for student dialogue and critical thinking. The district reports budgeting for AI initiatives in fiscal year 2027 and plans a one-year pilot with the instructional tool MagicSchool, expected to launch later in the fall.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The report states that CCSD is adopting a comprehensive, literacy-first AI policy built on its "safe, ethical, effective" framework, providing guidance rather than mandates and extending AI-literacy instruction to all grade levels, while district leaders maintain that AI should enhance but not replace teachers, dialogue, or students' independent thinking.

### 7. Rethinking Generative AI Literacy: An Integrative, Developmental, and Dialectical Framework for K–12 Teacher Education

1. **Title:** Rethinking Generative AI Literacy: An Integrative, Developmental, and Dialectical Framework for K–12 Teacher Education
2. **Publication date:** 3 August 2026 (preprint posted to arXiv; not peer-reviewed)
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** arXiv (preprint repository; identifier 2608.01705)
4. **Author(s):** Shahin Hossain, Sima Ahmadi, Leqi Li, Idowu David Awoyemi, Wei Huang, Chenxi Zhou, Jujia Li, Samaa Haniya, Shapla Khanam, and Tasbirun Mashreka Subaha
5. **Author note:** The authors are education and AI-literacy researchers publishing on generative AI in teacher education and K–12 settings.
6. **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01705
7. **Summary:**
This preprint addresses what the authors term a "GenAI literacy lag," in which generative AI has entered classrooms faster than teachers have been prepared to use it, so that technological diffusion outpaces educators' conceptual, pedagogical, and ethical readiness. To address the gap, the paper introduces the Responsible AI Literacy in Education (RAIL-Ed) framework, developed through a systematic review and qualitative framework analysis of 67 studies published between 2023 and 2025. The framework is presented as integrative, developmental, and dialectical, and is oriented specifically toward K–12 teacher education.

According to the paper's description, RAIL-Ed specifies a set of literacy pillars and a three-level rubric (Emerging, Competent, Advanced) describing how each pillar matures across the teacher-preparation continuum, intended to help teacher-education programs structure how educators build conceptual understanding, pedagogical application, and ethical judgment around generative AI. (This summary is based on the preprint's abstract and metadata; the full text was not independently retrieved during this run because the source could not be fetched.)
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The paper argues that current approaches to teacher AI preparation lag behind classroom adoption and proposes the RAIL-Ed framework as a structured, developmental model for building responsible generative-AI literacy in K–12 teacher education. As a non-peer-reviewed preprint, its framework is presented as a conceptual contribution derived from a review of prior studies rather than as an empirical evaluation.
