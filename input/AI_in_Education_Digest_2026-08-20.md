### 1. Data Literacy Skills Seen as Key for Success in the Age of AI

1. **Title:** Data Literacy Skills Seen as Key for Success in the Age of AI
2. **Publication date:** 11 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Week (Technology / Artificial Intelligence section)
4. **Author(s):** Jennifer Vilcarino
5. **Author note:** Vilcarino is a digital news reporter for Education Week covering K–12 technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.edweek.org/technology/data-literacy-skills-seen-as-key-for-success-in-the-age-of-ai/2026/08
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on a survey by Mathematica and Data Science for Everyone, conducted in February and March 2026 among 1,333 parents and educators in Colorado, Louisiana, and Tennessee, examining attitudes toward data literacy instruction in the context of artificial intelligence. It states that 88% of respondents said all students should be data literate before graduating high school, 76% said AI has made learning the skill more important, and 58% said schools currently provide opportunities to develop it. The piece quotes Zarek Drozda, executive director of Data Science for Everyone, who defines data literacy as teaching students to question, evaluate, interpret, and communicate information, and who argues that students must learn to question where AI tools' underlying data comes from, what may be wrong with it, and whether it is applied correctly.

The article notes structural obstacles to teaching these skills, reporting that data literacy is not mandated nationally: as of 2025, six states had adopted a data literacy learning standard, 11 included it in course catalogs, and 12 offered a pilot course or teacher professional development. It cites survey findings that 84% of educators and parents believe data literacy should be integrated across multiple subjects rather than only math or computer science, and 84% believe data skills are important for informed civic participation. It quotes Amber Zavicar, a K–12 math specialist in the Genesee Intermediate School District in Michigan, who describes students using data-science methods to examine questions including how AI use affects the brain, and who frames data science as a modern way of connecting mathematics to real-world questions.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that surveyed educators and parents view data literacy as an increasingly important skill in the age of AI while perceiving that schools do not provide enough opportunities to learn it, and that respondents favor integrating data literacy across subjects. It presents the sources' position that data literacy underpins critical thinking about AI-generated information and that decisions about integrating it are too often siloed by subject area.

### 2. Does Alpha School Work for Regular Kids?

1. **Title:** Does Alpha School Work for Regular Kids? (rendered in the page metadata as "Does the Alpha School Model Work for Regular Kids?")
2. **Publication date:** 12 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Mathworlds (Substack newsletter)
4. **Author(s):** Dan Meyer
5. **Author note:** Meyer is a math educator and author of the Mathworlds newsletter who analyzes the instructional logic of education technology; the post carries a disclosure that the views are his own and not endorsed by or affiliated with Amplify.
6. **URL:** https://danmeyer.substack.com/p/does-alpha-school-work-for-regular
7. **Summary:**
The post examines the Alpha School network of private schools (roughly a dozen campuses, tuition generally $40,000–$75,000) and its "2 Hour Learning" model, which claims students grow 2.6 times faster than peers on nationally normed MAP tests while spending about two hours a day on academics via an AI-driven platform, with teachers reframed as motivators and the rest of the day devoted to life-skills projects. Drawing on Alpha application pages and founder interviews, the post catalogs the ways Alpha selects its students—affordable tuition, multi-hour "Shadow Days," an interview, engaged parents, students "functioning within a typical range of ability and independence," no prior disciplinary removals, and "coachability"—and poses the central question of whether the model *makes* successful students or *selects* them.

To separate the model from the selection process, the post turns to Unbound Academy, an Arizona public charter school approved in December 2024 and opened in Fall 2025 that names Alpha co-founder MacKenzie Price as its board president, uses the 2 Hour Learning model, and—as a public charter—must enroll students by residency rather than by selection and is free to families. The post reports that in its first full year (2025–26), Unbound Academy's Arizona state assessment results underperformed its own charter-application projections, the state averages, and its stated baselines: roughly 10% math proficiency against a projected 60% (baseline 34%, state average 34%) and roughly 28% ELA proficiency against a projected 65% (baseline 40%, state average 42%). The author notes caveats—Unbound's actual intake may have entered below the predicted baseline, and second-year scores will likely rise—and appends a correction reattributing a quotation from the admissions director to MacKenzie Price.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claim is that Unbound Academy, as a non-selective public application of the same 2 Hour Learning model, drastically underperformed the targets Alpha's own team set for it, and that this outcome—described as a quasi-experiment rather than a definitive test—indicates it is far harder to *make* successful students than to *select* them in advance. The author states the results should not be the last word on 2 Hour Learning but should be the loudest.

### 4. AGI: Education's Blindspot

1. **Title:** AGI: Education's Blindspot
2. **Publication date:** 14 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Disrupted: Teaching and Learning in An AI World (Substack newsletter)
4. **Author(s):** Stefan Bauschard
5. **Author note:** Bauschard is an educator, debate coach, and author who writes the Education Disrupted newsletter on AI's implications for teaching, learning, and schooling.
6. **URL:** https://stefanbauschard.substack.com/p/agi-educations-blindspot
7. **Summary:**
The post argues that most education debates about AI rest on an outdated premise—AI as a next-word-predicting chatbot that hallucinates—and that this understates what frontier systems can now do. It attributes the shift to three training changes (reinforcement learning with automated verifiers, actively sourced data captured from expert workflows, and test-time compute), which it says produce systems that form strategies, execute them over long horizons, and adapt when blocked. It then characterizes four typical school responses to AI—doing nothing, forming a committee, buying edtech, and teaching students to challenge AI output—and focuses on the last, which it calls the "smartest wrong answer," arguing that a curriculum built around catching AI errors trains students for a permanent junior-partner role relative to systems whose reliability is rising and that it says are often configured (top-tier, maximum-reasoning) to critique the user rather than to be audited.

The post contrasts education's posture with the frontier AI labs' reorganization around artificial general intelligence, citing OpenAI's published plan and a "Personal AGI" team, Anthropic's $200 million Economic Futures Research Fund, and Google DeepMind's AGI safety paper and course, and notes forecasts placing AGI within a few years. It reports that, as of mid-2026, 37 states plus Puerto Rico had issued K-12 AI guidance and roughly 71 bills across 27 states were being tracked, but argues these focus on cheating, data privacy, procurement, teacher training on approved tools, disclosure, and screen time rather than on what graduates should be able to do in a labor market containing systems more capable than they are. It proposes cultivating "authorship" and accountability through project-based learning, debate, design thinking, and interdisciplinary work.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post's central claim, summarized in its own line, is that "the labs have a plan for AGI" while "education has a guidance document about cheating." It argues the blindspot is not that schools are pro- or anti-AI but that they are regulating AI as a tool to be managed within the existing model of school, while the organizations building the technology are planning for cognitive systems that could exceed the capabilities of the graduates schools produce, and that educators should ask what a graduate should be able to do when the machine is better than they are.

### 5. Solving GCSE mocks with Comparative Judgement

1. **Title:** Solving GCSE mocks with Comparative Judgement (subtitle: "Double Marker — our newest project")
2. **Publication date:** 14 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** No More Marking (Substack newsletter)
4. **Author(s):** Daisy Christodoulou and Chris Wheadon
5. **Author note:** Christodoulou is Director of Education at No More Marking and a cognitive-science-oriented writer on assessment; Wheadon is a co-founder of No More Marking, an organization that provides comparative-judgement assessment.
6. **URL:** https://substack.nomoremarking.com/p/solving-gcse-mocks-with-comparative
7. **Summary:**
The post describes a new No More Marking assessment product called "Double Marker," developed for marking GCSE mock examinations in England. It situates the tool within the organization's decade of Comparative Judgement work (about 2,000 schools and roughly 3 million pieces of writing), noting that since 2025 it has offered AI judges alongside human judges to reduce judging time and provide feedback. The authors frame a tension: Comparative Judgement uses holistic judgments without a mark scheme and is, they argue, more accurate than mark-scheme marking, yet schools marking GCSE mocks want engagement with the exam boards' detailed mark schemes. Double Marker's design uses AI Comparative Judgement as a first marker that assigns every script a reliable mark, and a human teacher as a second marker who applies the approved mark scheme to a selected sample, with those human marks used to fine-tune and adjust the AI-derived marks.

The post reports a summer-term pilot with 14 secondary schools on Year 10 GCSE English Language and Literature mocks and details the workflow: barcoding and scanning paper booklets, judging each school independently and then using a 20% moderation sample to place schools on a unified scale, having teachers mark samples with the mark scheme blind to the AI mark, running an optimization routine to correct AI bias and distance relative to the human marks, flagging large discrepancies for a senior marker who retains final override authority, and returning marks plus AI feedback at pupil, class, school, and cohort levels within two weeks. It states the pilot was successful and that a larger, still-free project is planned for October–November 2026.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post presents Double Marker as an AI-plus-human assessment workflow in which AI Comparative Judgement provides the primary, reliable mark and human teachers using the exam board's mark scheme calibrate the scale and can override flagged marks, framed as reducing teachers' marking workload while retaining human oversight, mark-scheme alignment, and comparability across schools for GCSE mock marking.

### 7. Slow math: Kids may learn more when AI makes them review mistakes

1. **Title:** Slow math: Kids may learn more when AI makes them review mistakes
2. **Publication date:** 17 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The Hechinger Report (Proof Points column; also appeared in Mind/Shift)
4. **Author(s):** Jill Barshay and Kristin Fasiang
5. **Author note:** Barshay is a staff writer at The Hechinger Report who writes the Proof Points column on education research; Fasiang is a graduate student in computer science and learning sciences at Northwestern University who co-reported the story.
6. **URL:** https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-mastery-learning/
7. **Summary:**
The column reports on a randomized experiment involving more than 6,000 middle school students in Tennessee that tested four approaches to practicing fractions. Students were randomly assigned to receive either conventional computer-based instruction or the same software with an AI tutor; within each group, half were required to answer practice questions on the same skill correctly three times in a row after making a mistake. Students used researcher-built software similar to Khan Academy's videos and exercises for one 50-minute session, and took a 15-minute test a week later. Students who received AI tutoring combined with this "mastery learning" repetition scored about 3 percentage points higher than students receiving conventional computerized instruction; the article characterizes the advantage as small, and quotes lead author Philip Oreopoulos, an economist at the University of Toronto, cautioning against overstating the result while noting it may be early evidence that AI has some positive value relative to no AI.

The article explains the researchers' interpretation that the AI helped by walking students through their mistakes rather than showing a solution they could skim, and reports that students in the AI-plus-mastery group spent more time per question and were more likely to answer the next question correctly after an error. It notes limits: the benefit appeared mainly on the easiest fraction questions most similar to those practiced and did not extend to harder problems, and the intervention lasted only 50 minutes. The underlying working paper, "Making AI Tutoring Productive: Evidence from a Mastery-Based Math Practice Experiment," by researchers from the University of Toronto and the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton School, was scheduled to be circulated by the National Bureau of Economic Research on 17 August and had not yet been peer-reviewed; the authors provided The Hechinger Report a draft.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports that combining an AI tutor with a mastery-learning requirement produced a small improvement in middle schoolers' fraction performance relative to conventional computer instruction, concentrated on the easiest, most similar problems, and that AI tutoring alone was not the decisive factor. It conveys the researchers' framing that AI may help most when it slows students down and requires them to work through mistakes, while emphasizing the result is preliminary, based on a brief intervention, and not yet peer-reviewed.

### 8. How Alpha School Teaches Writing

1. **Title:** How Alpha School Teaches Writing
2. **Publication date:** 17 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Minding the Gap (Substack newsletter)
4. **Author(s):** Natalie Wexler
5. **Author note:** Wexler is an education writer focused on literacy and knowledge-building curricula and co-author, with Judith C. Hochman, of a book describing The Writing Revolution / Hochman Method of writing instruction.
6. **URL:** https://nataliewexler.substack.com/p/how-alpha-school-teaches-writing
7. **Summary:**
The post examines AlphaWrite, the writing-instruction app used by the Alpha School network (which relies on AI "tutors" to deliver academics in about two hours a day). Drawing on a detailed description in the "Austin Scholar" newsletter written by a recent Alpha graduate, Wexler reports that AlphaWrite closely resembles The Writing Revolution / Hochman Method—explicit instruction progressing from sentences to paragraphs to essays with substantial guided practice and targeted feedback—and that the graduate reported AlphaWrite users grew 3.6 times in language and 3.3 times in reading during 2025–26, compared with 1.8 and 2.2 times for non-users at the same campuses (figures based on NWEA MAP tests). She identifies one key departure: whereas the Hochman Method is designed to be embedded in curriculum content, AlphaWrite embeds writing instruction in students' personal interests (with suggested topics such as Fortnite, Harry Potter, social media, video games, and basketball).

Wexler argues that anchoring writing instruction in personal interests rather than curriculum content conflicts with cognitive science, invoking writing's role in retrieval practice and elaboration and studies indicating that writing about content students have read or studied enhances learning. She distinguishes "personalized pathways to a well-rounded curriculum" from "personalized content" (citing education writer Lisa Hansel), questions whether writing about video games transfers to academic tasks such as literary analysis, and characterizes the approach as part of a strain of anti-intellectualism in the Alpha model, while noting she has not visited an Alpha School and is trying to keep an open mind. Footnotes raise questions about the Austin Scholar account and about claims that the app is based on the Common Core.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Wexler's central claim is that although AlphaWrite adopts an evidence-based explicit-writing method resembling The Writing Revolution, its decision to anchor writing in students' personal interests rather than academic curriculum content forgoes the learning that writing about curriculum content produces, and that this reflects a broader de-emphasis of academic knowledge in the Alpha School model.

### 9. Survey: 5 Ways AI Is Cutting Into Students' Ability to Learn

1. **Title:** Survey: 5 Ways AI Is Cutting Into Students' Ability to Learn
2. **Publication date:** 18 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The 74 (the74million.org), News section
4. **Author(s):** Greg Toppo
5. **Author note:** Toppo is a senior writer at The 74 who covers K–12 education and education technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-5-ways-ai-is-cutting-into-students-ability-to-learn/
7. **Summary:**
The article reports on a nationally representative Common Sense Media survey of 1,017 U.S. teens ages 13 to 17, conducted in April and May 2026, on how adolescents use generative AI and how they perceive its effect on their thinking. It reports that 70% of teens now use AI for schoolwork, but fewer than one in three say a teacher has talked with them about using it safely and only one in four have discussed with a teacher how to judge whether AI output is accurate. Among AI users, 77% describe using it as a support tool for brainstorming, checking work, and feedback, while 63% report going straight to a chatbot for an answer; of those, 25% use the output as-is, 31% rewrite it in their own voice, and 35% "improve it with what they know." The article also reports that 38% of users say AI leads them to develop fewer original ideas and 39% feel they are missing out on learning, and it quotes former U.S. Education Secretary John King, an advisory board member of the nonprofit's Youth AI Safety Institute, who calls the reliance figure worrying and urges changes to assessment such as in-class writing and oral presentations.

The article presents additional findings and voices. It reports that among teens who use AI, 44% have had a tool blocked on a school device or network, and 59% of those simply switched to a personal device, and it quotes recent Staten Island Technical High School graduate Darren Lam on widespread classroom AI use and on his concern that restricting access can prevent students from learning to use the tools. It lists the "human skills" teens ranked most important for their future (55% working well with others; 50% fact-checking and critical thinking; 48% reading and understanding complex information), and reports the survey arrived alongside the launch of a Common Sense Digital Literacy & Well-Being Curriculum and an AI-and-student-well-being professional development course. It quotes Alex Kotran, CEO of the AI Education Project, arguing that addressing students' cognitive offloading requires training teachers to understand how AI works so they can adapt assignments.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The article reports the survey's central findings that a large majority of teens use AI for schoolwork, that many acknowledge it may reduce their original thinking and learning, that school AI bans frequently shift use to less-supervised personal devices, and that most students have not discussed safe or accurate AI use with a teacher. It conveys the sources' shared position that schools should respond by teaching AI literacy and redesigning assessment so that students demonstrate their own learning, and that doing so depends on preparing teachers to understand the technology.

### 10. Howard Gardner: How AI Will Force Us to 'Rethink' Education

1. **Title:** Howard Gardner: How AI Will Force Us to 'Rethink' Education
2. **Publication date:** 18 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Week (opinion blog, "Rick Hess Straight Up," Artificial Intelligence section)
4. **Author(s):** Rick Hess (interviewer/author); Howard Gardner (interviewee)
5. **Author note:** Hess is director of Education Policy Studies at the American Enterprise Institute and author of Education Week's "Rick Hess Straight Up" blog; Gardner is the Hobbs Research Professor of Cognition and Education at the Harvard Graduate School of Education, known for the theory of multiple intelligences.
6. **URL:** https://www.edweek.org/technology/opinion-howard-gardner-how-ai-will-force-us-to-rethink-education/2026/08
7. **Summary:**
The post is an edited interview in which Hess, who has argued that AI does not change what students need to learn, questions Gardner, who disagrees. Gardner describes how his interest in AI arose from seeing the technology synthesize large volumes of information and code lengthy student interviews, and connects AI to his framework of multiple intelligences, noting that his 2024 essay "Who Owns Intelligence?" discussed animal, plant, and artificial intelligence and that educators must now consider how these forms relate. He argues that AI will become so effective at what he calls the "disciplined," "synthesizing," and "creative" minds that performing these cognitive tasks will be "optional" for humans, while contending that the "respectful mind" and "ethical mind" should remain distinctly human.

Gardner outlines a scenario in which every child still masters reading, writing, mathematics, and basic coding in a school setting alongside peers and educators, but in which, from around age 10, students pursue their own interests with help from AI, peers, and coaches; he suggests well-designed AI could outperform even gifted instructors and points to interest-anchored, cross-disciplinary exploration in the tradition of John Dewey and Reggio Emilia. Hess raises objections drawn from the history of ed-tech disappointment and from concerns—citing Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge—about screens, social ties, and youth mental health. Gardner responds that he shares those concerns, that young people should still spend substantial time with peers of differing backgrounds, and that he favors a deep, broad academic grounding while arguing it has not been achieved; his closing advice to educators is to learn about AI, experiment, and deliberately decide what to "gift" or "consign" to AI.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The post presents Gardner's central claim that AI will handle many cognitive tasks so well that humans' performance of them becomes optional, and that education should therefore be rethought—retaining foundational instruction and in-person social learning while allowing interest-driven, AI-supported exploration after the basics are mastered. Hess maintains his skepticism that AI changes what students need to know, and the exchange is framed as a disagreement between the two rather than a shared conclusion.

It raises, rather than resolves, the question of what an AI-first future in schools actually costs and who ultimately bears that cost.

### 12. When AI Makes Young People Think for Themselves, Real Learning Happens

1. **Title:** When AI Makes Young People Think for Themselves, Real Learning Happens
2. **Publication date:** 19 August 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** The 74 (the74million.org), Opinion section
4. **Author(s):** Ayisha Irfan
5. **Author note:** Irfan is founder and CEO of Augmented Intelligence Advisory, a software and advisory company that works with K–12 schools on teaching students to think alongside AI; the piece is labeled opinion.
6. **URL:** https://www.the74million.org/article/when-ai-makes-young-people-think-for-themselves-real-learning-happens/
7. **Summary:**
The opinion essay recounts a one-week AI pilot the author ran over the summer at the Chicago headquarters of Forging Opportunities for Refugees in America, involving 22 newcomer students ages 11 to 17 from Afghanistan, Burma, Malaysia, and Syria, many still building literacy skills. The author describes configuring Gemini to question a student about her reading rather than supply answers; after initial frustration, the student engaged more, scored 100% on a next-day quiz, and became more deliberate in her work. Over the week, students read short passages about how their brains work and used AI in different modes each day—summarizing the reading, engaging in Socratic question-and-answer, or writing their own question first—followed by same-day and next-day quizzes.

The essay reports that same-day quiz scores were similar across conditions, but a day later students who had engaged in Socratic Q&A retained more, averaging 83% versus 67% for material the AI had summarized, with more than two-thirds saying the questioning mode helped them learn more. It reports that students' confidence in their AI study skills rose sharply but had almost no relationship to what they actually retained, which the author frames as feeling like learning versus actually learning. The author argues that AI tools cannot be judged by engagement or satisfaction alone and should be measured by what students retain after the tool is removed, notes that districts are projected to spend heavily on AI ed-tech contracts without clear evaluation criteria, and states that students who could benefit most from personalized learning often receive the least usable output, concluding that AI literacy must be taught in schools.

8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The essay's central claim is that AI supports learning when it is designed to make students actively think—such as through Socratic questioning—rather than to supply answers, and that the true measure of an AI tool's value is durable retention after the tool is gone, not engagement, satisfaction, or students' self-assessed confidence. It argues that schools must teach AI literacy so that all students, not only those with tech-savvy families, learn to use AI as a thinking partner.
