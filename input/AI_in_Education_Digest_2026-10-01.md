### 1. An AI Policy Isn't a Playbook

1. **Title:** An AI Policy Isn't a Playbook
2. **Publication date:** 30 September 2026 (release date confirmed via Education Week and National News Desk/KATV coverage dated 30 September; the report page itself shows no date)
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Center for Digital Thriving, Harvard Graduate School of Education
4. **Author(s):** Beck Tench, Emily Weinstein, Carrie James, with Allison Starks and Sara Konrath
5. **Author note:** The authors are affiliated with Harvard's Center for Digital Thriving, which researches young people's digital lives and develops resources for educators and families (individual credentials not verified beyond this affiliation).
6. **URL:** https://digitalthriving.gse.harvard.edu/an-ai-policy-isnt-a-playbook/
7. **Summary:**
The report draws on a nationally representative survey of more than 1,000 U.S. public school teachers and principals (spring 2025) and on more than 40 conversations with 12 educators and 31 youth aged 15–19 (April–June 2026, per Education Week). It examines how generative AI affects classroom relationships. Among teachers who described AI-related dilemmas, 74% cited cheating, as did 69% of principals. Teachers describe relying on intuition ("AI-dar") to detect AI use, and students describe fear of false accusation and concealing their use.
The report proposes five conversation-based classroom "plays" and three "mindshifts": from assuming to asking, from technology-centered to student-centered, and from adversarial to collaborative.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors argue that enforcement-centered AI policies erode trust, make student thinking less visible to teachers and push AI use out of sight. They recommend that educators coach rather than police, through open dialogue about values and gray areas. *Validity: mixed-methods institutional report (survey plus interviews, co-design component); not peer-reviewed; interview sample is small.*

### 2. Friend or foe? Evidence from the use of generative artificial intelligence in learning and teaching

1. **Title:** Friend or foe? Evidence from the use of generative artificial intelligence in learning and teaching
2. **Publication date:** 22 September 2026 (per the European Commission announcement and Eunews)
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** European Commission, Publications Office of the EU
4. **Author(s):** Not stated on the pages that could be opened
5. **Author note:** Not available; institutional publication of the European Commission.
6. **URL:** https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/e1317635-b62b-11f1-81de-01aa75ed71a1/language-en (announcement: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/two-commission-reports-show-impact-artificial-intelligence-and-digital-technologies-teaching-and)
7. **Summary:**
The report reviews evidence on generative AI in learning and teaching across the EU. Its scope is not limited to K–12: it includes 16–24-year-olds and higher education. It reports that about four in ten 16–24-year-olds in the EU used generative AI for formal education in 2025, and that 29.7% of EU teachers had taken AI-related professional development (TALIS 2024).
It reports performance gains where students engage actively with AI rather than relying on it passively, and notes that long-term effects on skills remain uncertain.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Whether generative AI helps learning depends on how it is used. The report states that teachers need substantial initial and continuous professional development and calls for large-scale European studies. *Validity: institutional evidence review; the methods section was not seen; the full record page returned an access error during verification, so details come from the Commission announcement and press coverage.*

### 3. Digital education at school in Europe 2026: Bridging gaps in access, teaching and learning

1. **Title:** Digital education at school in Europe 2026: Bridging gaps in access, teaching and learning
2. **Publication date:** 22 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** EACEA / Eurydice (European Commission)
4. **Author(s):** Eurydice network (institutional; individual authors not stated)
5. **Author note:** Eurydice is the European Commission's network that analyses and compares national education systems and policies.
6. **URL:** https://eurydice.eacea.ec.europa.eu/publications/digital-education-school-europe-2026-bridging-gaps-access-teaching-and-learning
7. **Summary:**
This comparative report covers 38 education systems (27 EU member states plus 11 partner systems), using policy data and ICILS 2023 results. It addresses governance, access, equity, curricula and teacher competence in digital education, with AI as one strand.
According to the Commission announcement and Eunews, nearly two-thirds of the systems address AI through strategies or guidelines, and about one-third of curricula include AI-related skills.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** Digital education policies exist in most systems, but implementation is inconsistent, and gaps in access and teacher support persist, including with respect to AI. *Validity: comparative policy report; descriptive, not causal; AI is only part of its scope.*

### 4. Young People's Views on AI and Education (Seldon–Bunting report)

1. **Title:** Young People's Views on AI and Education (also referenced by Forbes as "Britain's Greatest Education Opportunity Ever")
2. **Publication date:** 22 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Public First (polling and research consultancy, UK)
4. **Author(s):** Sir Anthony Seldon and Tim Bunting
5. **Author note:** Seldon is a historian and former headmaster of Brighton College, Wellington College and Epsom College; Bunting is a founding partner at the investment firm Balderton Capital.
6. **URL:** https://www.publicfirst.co.uk/seldon-bunting-report
7. **Summary:**
Public First polled 4,000 young people in England and added an international evidence review. It reports that 87% have used AI tools (90% among 13–14-year-olds), with use at 92% in the highest social grades versus 79% in the lowest. 42% say AI makes school less important, and 9% say schools prepare them well for AI. 47% versus 21% say they would trust AI over a person to check a fact.
The report includes a ten-point action plan for government. The age range extends beyond K–12, and the commissioning body is not stated.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors argue that schools are unprepared for AI and that a digital divide exists, and recommend, among other measures, mandatory school AI strategies backed by regulator penalties (as characterised in coverage). *Validity: opinion poll plus advocacy report; self-reported data; England-only sample.*

### 5. Will It Teach as Intended? How Teachers Configure Educational AI Chatbots

1. **Title:** Will It Teach as Intended? How Teachers Configure Educational AI Chatbots
2. **Publication date:** 24 September 2026 (arXiv v1)
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** arXiv (preprint); authors from NC State University, Kennesaw State University, University of Virginia and Georgia Tech
4. **Author(s):** Bahare Riahi, Deniz Ozturk, Alice Guth, Jiayu Li, Daksh Pratap Singh, Xiaoyi Tian, Jennifer Chiu, Nicholas Lytle, Tiffany Barnes, Veronica Cateté
5. **Author note:** The authors are researchers at U.S. universities working on computing education, learning technologies and K–12 teacher professional development (affiliations only; individual roles not verified).
6. **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29993v1
7. **Summary:**
This mixed-methods study involved 27 middle school teachers at two U.S. sites during summer 2026 professional development workshops. Teachers defined the purpose, rules, persona and tone of an educational AI chatbot. The researchers analysed focus groups, configuration logs and chatbot interaction logs.
Alignment between teacher intent and chatbot behaviour was 88.9% for responsiveness and 81.5% for persona, versus 70.4% for rules and 59.3% for purpose.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The authors conclude that configurable controls alone do not ensure that a chatbot behaves as the teacher intended, and that teachers need better tools to express, test and refine intended chatbot behaviour. *Validity: preprint, not peer-reviewed; small convenience sample from workshops.*

### 6. Schools are experimenting with AI with little evidence or policy to guide them

1. **Title:** Schools are experimenting with AI with little evidence or policy to guide them
2. **Publication date:** 28 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** NPR (read via KPBS station mirror; also carried by Texas Public Radio and others)
4. **Author(s):** Lee V. Gaines
5. **Author note:** Gaines is a freelance education reporter for NPR covering AI in classrooms (per the KPBS mirror).
6. **URL:** https://www.kpbs.org/news/education/2026/09/28/schools-are-experimenting-with-ai-with-little-evidence-or-policy-to-guide-them (canonical: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/28/nx-s1-5759718/ai-schools-experiment-research)
7. **Summary:**
The story reports that U.S. districts are adopting AI tools quickly with little supporting research. It opens at an alternative school in Dayton, Ohio, where students talk to an AI chatbot, and describes teachers using AI for lesson planning and grading and some districts using AI tutors or robots. It cites a Stanford review of more than 800 academic papers that found very little research on AI's effect on student learning.
District leaders say they struggle to define success and mostly rely on perception-based feedback. Salamanca City Central School District dropped a plan for an AI robot after data-privacy concerns, and New York City and Los Angeles Unified have restricted student-facing AI, especially for younger students. Experts quoted warn of "cognitive stunting" and dependence.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, AI adoption is outpacing evidence, effectiveness is hard to measure, federal and state leadership is largely absent, and some districts are pulling back after community pushback; quoted experts favour targeted, problem-specific pilots over broad rollouts. *Validity: news report citing secondary research. The NPR page was not directly fetchable; content is from the KPBS mirror.*

### 7. School districts, slowly, build guidance around AI learning in classrooms

1. **Title:** School districts, slowly, build guidance around AI learning in classrooms
2. **Publication date:** 22 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** WBUR (Boston public radio)
4. **Author(s):** Suevon Lee
5. **Author note:** Lee is an education correspondent at WBUR.
6. **URL:** https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/09/22/massachusetts-schools-ai-use
7. **Summary:**
The article reports that Massachusetts districts are writing their own AI policies rather than banning the technology. Boston, Lexington and Medfield allow uses such as translation, persuasive-writing practice and summarizing, and restrict others, such as generating essay ideas or self-reflections without teacher approval. It contrasts this with broader restrictions in New York City and Los Angeles.
It cites figures that nearly one in five students aged 9–17 use AI daily for schoolwork, mostly ChatGPT, and about half report no instruction on AI accuracy. It also reports a state education department guidance framework, philanthropy-funded teacher training in Boston, and a proposed AI-literacy graduation requirement with no timeline.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, Massachusetts districts favour enabling responsible use over bans, policy clarity varies widely between districts, and teacher training and AI literacy are emerging priorities. *Validity: news report.*

### 8. Can Schools Trust AI to Translate for Non-English-Speaking Parents?

1. **Title:** Can Schools Trust AI to Translate for Non-English-Speaking Parents?
2. **Publication date:** 21 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Education Week
4. **Author(s):** Alyson Klein
5. **Author note:** Klein is an assistant editor at Education Week covering education policy and technology.
6. **URL:** https://www.edweek.org/technology/can-schools-trust-ai-to-translate-for-non-english-speaking-parents/2026/09
7. **Summary:**
The article notes that about one in five U.S. children speak another language at home, and that districts use free tools (Google Translate, ChatGPT) and paid apps with translation features for family communication. It reports that AI translation can struggle with nuance, tone, idiom and lower-resource languages, with examples such as "senior" rendered as "senior citizen" and a garbled Spanish version of a driving-line notice.
It reports that privacy requires data-sharing agreements, citing a New York district that vetted its app, and that experts advise human review of sensitive documents, plain-language source text and transparency about AI use.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** As reported, AI translation can aid communication but is imperfect; human oversight should scale with the stakes, and special education documents should not rely on machine translation alone. *Validity: news report / explainer.*

### 9. Are Governments Failing Schools When It Comes To AI?

1. **Title:** Are Governments Failing Schools When It Comes To AI?
2. **Publication date:** 25 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Forbes (contributor column)
4. **Author(s):** Dan Fitzpatrick
5. **Author note:** Fitzpatrick is a keynote speaker on AI, education and leadership and a Forbes contributor (per his Forbes profile); the source list notes a commercial interest in AI-for-educators products.
6. **URL:** https://www.forbes.com/sites/danfitzpatrick/2026/09/25/are-governments-failing-schools-when-it-comes-to-ai/
7. **Summary:**
The column reports that Sir Anthony Seldon warned that schools are not preparing students for an AI-driven future, citing the Seldon–Bunting poll (87% of young people use AI; 9% think schools prepare them well; see item 4). Seldon and Tim Bunting call for an AI strategy in every school and regulator penalties for inaction. The UK government's reply pointed to curriculum updates, AI tutoring programmes and £23 million for edtech testing.
Fitzpatrick describes these responses as "linear innovation," meaning incremental gains within current structures, and contrasts them with "nonlinear" redesign of timetables, teaching roles and assessment. He cites Alpha School's compressed academic model and Houston ISD's Future 2 schools as nonlinear experiments, noting they are mostly open to wealthy families or specific districts.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The author argues that governments take AI seriously but largely protect existing school models, and that the central equity question is whether public systems will lead transformative change for all children or whether next-generation schooling will be available only to those who can pay. *Validity: opinion piece.*

### 10. AI Won't Break Education. Failing to Prepare Teachers Might.

1. **Title:** AI Won't Break Education. Failing to Prepare Teachers Might.
2. **Publication date:** 28 September 2026
3. **Institution / publisher / platform:** Efekta Education (education company), via PR Newswire
4. **Author(s):** No individual author; survey conducted by 3Gem Research & Insights; Dr. Stefania Giannini and Dr. Christopher McCormick are quoted
5. **Author note:** Giannini is identified in the release as Efekta's President of Government Relations and McCormick as its Chief Academic Advisor (per the release).
6. **URL:** https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/ai-wont-break-education-failing-to-prepare-teachers-might-302890928.html
7. **Summary:**
The release reports a survey of 850 teachers (500 U.S., 350 UK) fielded 24 June–2 July 2026. In the U.S., 73% use AI tools, 72% of those use ChatGPT, and 19% use education-specific tools. 50% report formal training, 70% feel encouraged by their schools, and 40% say their school has integrated AI successfully. 93% know of or suspect student AI use for coursework, 44% say AI was not designed with their needs in mind, and nearly half call government guidance unclear.
The company recommends clearer government policy, practical school frameworks, professional development, evaluation of education-specific tools and teacher input in tool selection.
8. **Main conclusions / central claims:** The company argues that gaps in teacher preparation, rather than the technology itself, are the larger risk, and that AI should support rather than replace teacher judgment. *Validity: vendor-commissioned survey in a press release; full methods and weighting not seen.*
